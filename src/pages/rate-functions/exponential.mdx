# Exponential family

The exponential family is the genre's pacing primitive. Where the
[power-law family](/rate-functions/power-law) covers "rate is some power of state," the exponential
family covers "rate compounds." Each unit of $x$ multiplies the rate by a fixed factor;
the cumulative grows at the same rate; the first-passage in $C$ is logarithmic. Two notations,
discrete and continuous, point at the same family.

## Geometric (discrete)

$$a_n = a \cdot m^n, \quad S_n = a \cdot \frac{m^{n+1} - 1}{m - 1}, \quad N(C) = \log_m\!\left(\frac{C(m-1)}{a} + 1\right) - 1$$

The per-purchase cost ladder. $a$ is the cost of the zeroth purchase; $m$ is the
cost ratio between consecutive purchases. The
[partial sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_series) $S_n$ is a closed form known
since [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes); what makes the geometric
shape the genre's default cost primitive is that the inverse — "how many can I afford with
budget $C$?" — is also elementary.

The lead corpus exemplar is Cookie Clicker, which uses $m = 1.15$ across every building:

<Cite repo="cookie-clicker" path="main.js" line="7972" />

```js
Game.priceIncrease = 1.15;
```

<Cite repo="cookie-clicker" path="main.js" line="8090-8094" />

```js
this.getPrice = function (n) {
  var price = this.basePrice * Math.pow(Game.priceIncrease, Math.max(0, this.amount - this.free));
  price = Game.modifyBuildingPrice(this, price);
  return Math.ceil(price);
};
```

That cost ratio — $1.15$ — became the genre default, adopted by later games with little
renegotiation. The reason the choice stuck is the doubling time:
$(\log_2 1.15)^{-1} \approx 4.96$, so each five purchases double the cost. A run that climbs
through fifty purchases of a building has gone through ten doublings, $1024\times$ over the
base cost. A run that climbs through a hundred has gone through twenty, $\approx 10^6\times$.
The same formula holds at every scale; the _cost ratio_ behind the difficulty escalation is constant.

Antimatter Dimensions ships an engineered version — a parameterized cost-scaling class
with both a max-quantity-affordable and a price-at-quantity, closed-form below its
threshold because the shape there is geometric:

<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/math.js" line="320-352" />

```js
window.ExponentialCostScaling = class ExponentialCostScaling {
  // baseCost B, baseIncrease C, costScale S, purchasesBeforeScaling n.
  // Pre-scaling: B, B·C, B·C², ..., B·C^n.
  // Post-scaling: B·C^(n+1), B·C^(n+2)·S, B·C^(n+3)·S^3, ...
};
```

The doc-comment's price ladder is two regimes glued together: a pure geometric
$B \cdot C^k$ before the scaling threshold, then a shape past it whose step ratio grows by
$S$ with every purchase. That second regime has left this family — it is
[super-exponential](#super-exponential), covered at the end of this page — and the glue
between the two is a [piecewise splice](/rate-functions/composition#piecewise-splice). AD's late-game
cost climbs this way to give the player two distinct "speed" regimes.

<GeometricRateDemo client:visible />

The doubling-time readout updates as you slide $m$. At $m = 1.15$ the doubling time is
about $5$ purchases, the genre default. At $m = 1.05$ doubling takes ~14 purchases, a markedly slower case. At $m = 1.30$ the case is harsh:
each three purchases double the cost. Designers who pick a cost ratio are picking the
slope of the late-game wall.

## Continuous (exponential)

$$r(x) = a \cdot e^{b x}, \quad R(x) = \frac{a}{b}\!\left(e^{b x} - 1\right), \quad T(C) = \frac{1}{b} \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{b C}{a}\right)$$

The same family in continuous-time notation. The relationship $m = e^b$ — equivalently
$b = \ln m$ — is what makes the two readings interchangeable. A geometric ladder with
ratio $m = 1.15$ is a continuous exponential with $b = \ln(1.15) \approx 0.1398$;
[doubling time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubling_time) in $x$ is $(\ln 2)/b$, the
same number as $(\log_2 m)^{-1}$ rounded to the same place.

Why both notations exist:

- **Discrete** maps directly to "cost of the $n$-th purchase," which is the player-facing
  quantity in any clicker built around buying buildings. The sum $S_n$ tells you the total
  spent; the inverse $N(C)$ tells you how many you can afford with budget $C$. No calculus
  needed; the formulas are arithmetic.
- **Continuous** maps directly to "production rate at time $t$," which is the relevant
  reading when production itself is exponential — feedback loops, prestige multipliers,
  compound growth. The cumulative is the integral; the first-passage is the inverse. Calculus
  works.

Most pages in this catalog and the rest of the site move freely between the two notations
because the underlying family is one. The demo above is a continuous evaluation under the
hood; the per-purchase reading lines up exactly with $m = e^b$.

## What the exponential family is for

The genre uses exponential cost paired with linear (or polynomial) production — Pecorella's
Part I seesaw — because the _ratio_ of cost to production grows exponentially while the
production itself only grows polynomially. That ratio is the beat time, and
its growth is what makes early progress feel fast and late progress feel slow without any
explicit pacing rule. The math is doing the pacing.

Where this breaks down is exactly where modern incrementals went looking for new
primitives. An unmodified geometric ladder with $m = 1.15$ produces decade-spanning
late-game runs that the genre's late-2010s production substrate (`break_infinity`,
`break_eternity`) had to be engineered to handle — past $1e308$ the naïve `Math.pow` form
overflows, and the exponential family's natural endpoint is somewhere past that. Big-number
arithmetic (covered on the [big numbers page](/modern/big-numbers)) is what made the
exponential family practical past the JavaScript Number limit; before then, the late game hit a wall not
because the math broke but because the floats did.

The other direction the genre went is _softening_ the exponential past a threshold —
[piecewise splices](/rate-functions/composition#piecewise-splice) of geometric with a slower geometric, geometric
with a polynomial, geometric with a logarithmic shape. Each is a way to reach the
post-doubling regime without committing to the exponential's full late-game blowup. The
[softcaps page](/modern/softcaps) catalogs the design patterns; the math underneath is
piecewise splice of this entry's family with the others.

## Asymptotic concentration

A clean fact about the geometric cumulative that's worth stating explicitly:

$$\frac{\sum_{n=N-K+1}^{N} a_n}{\sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n} = \frac{m^N - m^{N-K}}{m^N - 1} \;\xrightarrow{N \to \infty}\; 1 - m^{-K}$$

The last $K$ purchases of any geometric cumulative account for approximately $1 - m^{-K}$ of the total,
independent of $N$ once $N$ is large. For $m = 1.15$ and $K = 10$, that's $\approx 0.753$ —
the last 10 purchases account for 75% of the integral. For $K = 5$, $\approx 0.503$ — the last 5
account for half.

<TailConcentrationDemo client:visible />

The bar chart makes the asymptote visible. At any $m > 1.05$, the first 40 of 50 bars
collapse into a thin strip on the left while the last few dominate the picture; sliding
$N$ from 30 to 100 barely changes the shaded share because the cumulative is already
saturated by its tail. The closed form $(m^N - m^{N-K})/(m^N - 1)$ converges to
$1 - m^{-K}$ at a rate set by the doubling time — past three or four doublings of the
range, the exact share and the asymptote are visually indistinguishable.

This is the math behind the genre's "the last few purchases are everything" feeling. It's not
a perception artifact; it's the underlying rate-function shape. Every softcap, every
pacing tweak, every "milestone bonus that activates at purchase 25" is operating against this
concentration: the question is whether the late-game work is meaningful or whether it
collapses into a single 75%-of-total wait.

## Super-exponential

$$a_n \;\text{ with }\; \rho(n) = \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \to \infty$$

Some cost ladders outgrow every geometric: the step ratio itself keeps rising. The shipped
forms are $b^{n^p}$ with $p > 1$ (log-quadratic $b^{n^2}$ is the common one), $n^n$ and its
relatives, [factorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factorial)-style products, and the
double exponential $b^{b^n}$. None has an elementary partial sum. None needs one. If $\rho$
is a ratio no larger than every step ratio up to $N$, the sum is bracketed by its last term:

$$a_N \;\le\; S_N \;\le\; a_N \cdot \frac{\rho}{\rho - 1}$$

For a geometric ladder $\rho = m$ is constant, so the total stays a fixed multiple of the
last term — the concentration result above, read at $K = 1$. When the ratios grow, that
multiple goes to 1: $S_N / a_N \to 1$. The cumulative _is_ its last term, so first-passage
is the inverse of one term, accurate to within one purchase once the ratio passes 2.

| Cost $a_n$         | Inverse of one term                                                                 |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $b^{n^p}$, $p > 1$ | $(\log_b C)^{1/p}$                                                                  |
| $n^n$              | $\ln C \,/\, W(\ln C)$, through [Lambert W](/rate-functions/log-frontier)            |
| $n!$               | the inverse gamma function; [Stirling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling%27s_approximation) for an estimate |
| $b^{b^n}$          | $\log_b \log_b C$                                                                   |

There is no fixed purchase count per doubling: each doubling of cost arrives in fewer purchases than the
last. That is the whole design appeal — a wall that steepens on its own — and the
[cost-acceleration page](/modern/cost-acceleration) walks the history of games reaching for
it.

Dimensional Incremental Rewind prices dots at $2^{(x/\text{mult})^2}$, and its gain function
is exactly the term inverse, $\sqrt{\log_2 y} \cdot \text{mult}$ — the game never sums the
ladder, because it doesn't have to:

The other side of geometric is the stretched exponential, $b^{n^p}$ with $p < 1$. Its ratio
falls toward 1, the last term stops dominating, and the cumulative becomes an
[incomplete gamma function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incomplete_gamma_function) — bounded
by comparison with the integral rather than inverted term by term.
