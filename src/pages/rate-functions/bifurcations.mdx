# Bifurcations: where the algebra ends

The first six pages of this catalog walked shapes that close: power-law,
exponential, logistic, log, and the composition rules that keep them closed
under sums, products, splices, functional composition, and quantization. This
page walks the complement — the rate-function shapes that don't close, and what
the spec layer reaches for instead. The catalog stops where the algebra stops;
past that, the math doesn't disappear, it changes language.

The first-passage frame survives every bifurcation. Each row below is still
"time to reach a milestone" in some generalized sense — mean hitting time,
equilibrium, threshold policy, optimum over a discrete option space. What
changes is the algebra of "rate" and the meaning of "cumulative." Each row
also has a body of named mathematics that gives a different shape of spec —
not "give up and simulate."

## The eight buckets

| Bucket                         | What fails to close                                                     | Named adjacent math                            |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Coupled-state deterministic    | Multi-resource mutual feedback, neither linear nor triangular           | Phase plane, Lyapunov, bifurcation theory      |
| Strategy-space integration     | An equilibrium between agents' strategies                               | Nash, replicator dynamics, combinatorial games |
| Stochastic load-bearing        | The distribution shape carries the design intent, not the mean          | Markov chains, phase-type, first-passage       |
| Network-topological            | Throughput bounded by graph topology, not rates                         | Max-flow min-cut, LP duality, network flows    |
| Bilevel interdiction           | A placement chosen against a follower who re-optimizes                  | Stackelberg/bilevel programs, interdiction     |
| Optimal stopping               | When to reset is itself the decision                                    | Bellman equation, Snell envelope, free-boundary |
| Combinatorial selection        | A configuration chosen from a discrete option space                     | Combinatorial optimization, constraint satisfaction |
| Discrete planning / scheduling | An ordered action sequence whose steps change the state later steps read | AI planning, scheduling, MDPs                  |

A bucket earns its row three ways: named mathematics with its own spec form, a
test against each neighboring bucket that can be checked in a game's source,
and at least one shipped subsystem read at source. Each section below ends with
those tests — its discriminators.

A subsystem can land in two buckets. The common pair is coupled-state per fixed
strategy and optimal stopping across strategies; name both, because they call
for different tools.

How the runtime resolves a subsystem is not a bucket. Tick-resolved combat,
trigger chains, and auto-battler rounds say whether the game integrates its spec
continuously or steps through events. The spec itself lands wherever its math
does: usually closed-form, stochastic load-bearing when targets or rolls are
random, planning when the player orders the chain.

## Coupled-state deterministic

When two or more resources mutually feed each other's rates and the coupling is
neither linear nor triangular — predator–prey, multi-species
[Lotka-Volterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka%E2%80%93Volterra_equations),
multi-faction warfare — the rate-function algebra runs out. A closed-form
trajectory often still exists implicitly through a
[conserved quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law), but the
time-parametrization $t(R)$ — when the orbit reaches a given point — does not.

Coupling needs two stocks. A rate that reads only its own stock is a scalar
ODE, $dx/dt = f(x)$, and its first-passage $T = \int dx / f(x)$ closes whenever
that integral does.

The spec layer in this regime drops down to qualitative invariants:
[matrix exponentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_exponential) for the
linear case, [phase portraits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_portrait) and
[Lyapunov functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyapunov_function) for stable
equilibria, [bifurcation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifurcation_theory)
for parameter-driven shape changes. Most idle/incremental games avoid genuinely
coupled multi-resource systems for exactly this reason — the games that ship
them either restrict to linear dynamics or simplify the coupling to a
triangular dependency that closes per the
[composition rules](/rate-functions/composition#what-doesnt-compose-cleanly).

DodecaDragons ships the coupling in its first two resources. Gold's rate is
$\text{miners} \cdot (1 + 2\log_{10}(1 + \text{fire}/10))$, fire's rate
multiplies by $1 + k\log_{10}(\text{gold} + 1)$, and both stocks accrue every
tick:

<Cite repo="DodecaDragons" path="scripts/script.js" line="1313" />
<Cite repo="DodecaDragons" path="scripts/script.js" line="1387-1395" />

Each rate is a log of the other stock, so neither closes without the other's
trajectory.

The textbook coupled-state system is Lotka-Volterra predator–prey. Slide the
parameters to watch closed orbits trace around the equilibrium $(\gamma/\delta,
\alpha/\beta)$ — the catalog's first-passage time still exists (return to
$y_0$), but no elementary algebra delivers it.

**Discriminators.**

- _vs. closed-form_ — does any modifier read the state it modifies? No, and the
  [composition rules](/rate-functions/composition) close it. One stock reading
  itself is the scalar ODE above.
- _vs. stochastic load-bearing_ — remove every random draw. If the design intent
  survives, the subsystem is here.
- _vs. network-topological_ — does a graph's capacity bound the throughput? Yes,
  and it's network. Feedback through stocks with no capacity bound is here.

## Strategy-space integration

When a "rate" depends on what an opponent does — tug-of-war faction warfare,
PvP-idle drafting from a shared pool — the per-fixed-strategy ROI closes per
the algebra, but the equilibrium between strategies is a fixed point of the
[best-response operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_response):
$\sigma^* = BR(\sigma^*)$. Fixed points of operators are not in general
closed-form, even when the operator is. Outside small special cases (zero-sum,
potential, supermodular games), Nash computation is
[PPAD-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PPAD_(complexity)).

The spec layer shifts to the *game form* — strategy spaces, payoff functions,
information structure — and a claim about which equilibrium concept applies:
[Nash equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_equilibrium) when unique,
[replicator dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replicator_equation) from a
uniform start when not. The bucket is rare in the genre because most idle games
are cooperative or single-player; it activates with adversarial multi-agent
strategy spaces over shared resource draws.

Turn-based two-player games sit here too. A perfect-information zero-sum game
has a value by [minimax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimax) — in principle,
at combinatorial cost, since the state space grows exponentially in units or
cards. [Combinatorial game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_game_theory)
supplies the equivalence classes, and the
[Sprague-Grundy theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sprague%E2%80%93Grundy_theorem)
reduces a sum of impartial games to a single nim-value. The spec is the rule-set
as a state graph, plus value bounds and any dominant strategy.

Replicator dynamics on Rock-Paper-Scissors visualizes the regime split:
zero-sum produces closed orbits around the centroid (the Nash equilibrium of
the mixed game), and even small perturbations of the diagonal payoff flip the
centroid between attractor and repeller.

<BestResponseDynamics client:visible />

**Discriminators.**

- _vs. bilevel interdiction_ — is the solution a fixed point where no agent
  wants to deviate? Yes, and it's here. One side commits and the other answers,
  with no fixed point sought, is interdiction.
- _vs. combinatorial selection and planning_ — does another agent's choice enter
  the payoff? Yes, and it's here. One agent against fixed rules is selection or
  planning.
- _vs. stochastic load-bearing_ — the uncertainty is another agent's choice,
  not a draw from a fixed distribution.

## Stochastic load-bearing

When a production mechanic's *distribution shape* — not just its mean — carries
the design intent. Mean first-passage and stationary distribution close for
finite [Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain) (the linear
system $Q \cdot m = -1$); the full distribution of first-passage times rarely
does, even for a random walk hitting a barrier (the hitting-time distribution
involves [Bessel functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_function)).

The spec is the distribution family — three named ones cover most of the
genre's territory:
[geometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_distribution) for trials
until first success (gacha pity timers' natural model);
[exponential / gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_distribution) for
continuous-time waits in Poisson-like systems;
[phase-type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase-type_distribution) for
finite-state continuous-time Markov-chain hitting times, flexible enough to
cover most game-mechanic randomness with closed-form mean, variance, and
Laplace transform. The dimensionless feel-knob is the
[coefficient of variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_variation)
$\sigma/\mu$.

Universal Paperclips prices wire this way. On a random 1.5% of ticks the price
takes one step along a sinusoid around a slowly decaying base, so the player
times purchases against a noisy price — the shape of the noise, not its mean, is
what they play against:

<Cite repo="paperclips" path="main.js" line="23-37" />

Combat with random targeting lands here when its outcome distribution is the
design intent. The spec is the outcome distribution per army composition — a
vector-matrix product over a finite event lattice, which the combat loop
samples at runtime.

The headline is that mean and median diverge as CV grows. Geometric pulls
without pity have a long tail; pity at $K$ collapses the tail and barely
moves the mean. Erlang-$k$ runs $\text{CV} = 1/\sqrt{k}$ — peaked-at-zero at
$k=1$, near-Gaussian by $k=10$. Hyperexponential mixtures push CV past 1.

**Discriminators.**

- _vs. coupled-state deterministic_ — replace each draw with its mean. If the
  design intent survives, the subsystem is coupled-state or closed-form, not
  here.
- _vs. strategy-space integration_ — the randomness is nature's draw from a
  fixed distribution, not an agent's choice.
- _vs. discrete planning / scheduling_ — does the intent sit in the outcome
  distribution, or in the order the player chooses? The order is planning, with
  [Markov decision processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_decision_process)
  for the random transitions.

## Network-topological

When throughput is bounded by graph topology, not by per-rate formulas — supply
chains, factory wiring, RAM allocation across a server network. The linear case
closes:
[max-flow equals min-cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max-flow_min-cut_theorem)
by [linear programming duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duality_(optimization)),
and [Dijkstra's algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dijkstra%27s_algorithm)
for shortest paths runs in polynomial time. The two failure modes are nonlinear
costs (capacity or cost depending on flow — congestion games, Wardrop equilibria
push the problem into NLP) and combinatorial constraints (integer-valued flows
in NP-hard problems admit only approximations and bounds).

The spec layer is the graph and capacities (or capacity functions) plus the
optimality criterion. The first-passage analog reads as "time to deliver $C$
units from source to sink given throughput bound $\text{cap}_{\min}$" — for a
saturated network, throughput is the min-cut value, and design knobs are the
graph topology and per-edge capacities.

Upload Labs is the clearest exemplar. The player wires windows into a graph. A
window's output rate is the minimum of its own rate and each input's production
per unit it consumes, and a container splits its stock and production equally
across its outgoing wires:

<Cite repo="upload-labs" path="scenes/windows/window_single_file_process.gd" line="45-49" />
<Cite repo="upload-labs" path="scenes/resource_container.gd" line="99-123" />

That equal split is a routing rule, and the routing rule decides which flow the
player gets. Max-flow is what an optimal router achieves; a rule that splits
evenly delivers less whenever the branches differ in capacity. So the spec
names the routing rule beside the graph.

A small five-node graph makes the theorem felt: edge thickness shows flow
share, the saturated min-cut is highlighted, and total throughput under an
optimal router equals the cut's capacity. Bumping a non-cut edge does nothing;
bumping a cut edge moves throughput one-for-one until the cut shifts elsewhere.

**Discriminators.**

- _vs. bilevel interdiction_ — does anyone re-optimize against the graph the
  player builds? No, and it's here. An enemy re-routing around the player's
  blockers is interdiction.
- _vs. coupled-state deterministic_ — is throughput capped by an edge or node
  capacity? Yes, and it's here.
- _vs. discrete planning / scheduling_ — a route the player walks step by step,
  changing state as it goes, is a plan. A standing flow through a graph is here.

## Bilevel interdiction

When the player's decision is not a route through the graph but a *constraint on
someone else's* — place budget-bounded blockers, and a follower re-solves its
own shortest path against whatever stays open. Maze-building tower defense is
the genre's carrier: the enemy path is not authored, it is whatever survives the
walls.

Per fixed blocker set the inner problem closes cheaply — a distance field over
the open cells, polynomial, exact. What fails to close is the outer choice. The
leader picks a blocker set $B$ within budget, the follower answers with $\min$
over paths, and the design objective is the max-min:

$$\max_{B\,:\,c(B)\le b} \;\; \min_{p \in P(B)} \; \ell(p)$$

Two nested optimizations with opposed objectives is a
[bilevel program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilevel_optimization), and this
particular one — shortest-path network interdiction, the $k$-most-vital-nodes
problem — is NP-hard even though its inner problem is textbook.

The spec layer is four statements: the blocker budget and its cost schedule;
the follower's objective and what counts as passable to it; the feasibility
constraint, usually that every spawn stays connected to the goal
([Menger's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menger%27s_theorem) says a
placement is illegal exactly when it separates them); and the acceptance
criterion, in path length or in time to cross. That last reads as
$T(b) = \ell^*(b)/v$, nondecreasing in the budget and saturating at the
unbudgeted maximum $\ell^*(\infty)$ — a longest-simple-path ceiling on the open
region, which grades a funded greedy against what unlimited money could buy.

With those written down, a greedy that places one blocker at a time is the
*fast solver* rather than the definition of the mechanic, and the spec says
where it is blind. On boards with a plateau, no single blocker lengthens the
path and two placed together do, so marginal scoring reads "nothing left to
buy." The exact method generates one constraint per incumbent path — "block at
least one cell of this one" — and re-solves, which handles plateaus of any
width. Its separation oracle evaluates only cells on the standing path, a
restriction implementations reach independently.

GemCraft is the clearest exemplar, and unusually explicit about it. A wall is a
pure blocker — the class carries a grid position and an empty per-frame
handler, no damage, no range — so the routing decision is separated from the
coverage decision in the game's own type system.

**Discriminators.**

- _vs. network-topological_ — a follower re-solves against the player's
  structure. Max-flow closes by LP duality; interdiction's
  [linear relaxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_programming_relaxation)
  has an integrality gap wide enough that the bound it returns grades nothing.
- _vs. strategy-space integration_ — a
  [Stackelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stackelberg_competition) leader
  commits first and the follower answers. That resolves by decomposition;
  nobody looks for a fixed point.
- _vs. combinatorial selection_ — evaluating one configuration contains a second
  optimizer, the follower's path solve. A selection's per-configuration
  evaluation is a formula.

## Optimal stopping

When *when to reset* is itself the decision; the value function is the supremum
over [stopping times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stopping_time):

$$V(x) = \sup_\tau \mathbb{E}\!\left[ e^{-\rho \tau} \cdot g(X_\tau) \mid X_0 = x \right]$$

Threshold policies close in special cases —
[geometric Brownian motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_Brownian_motion)
with discounted exponential payoffs, finite-horizon discounted MDPs with
special structure,
[Gittins indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gittins_index) for multi-armed
bandits with discounting. The general case is a
[free-boundary problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_boundary_problem) on
the [Bellman equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellman_equation), with
the optimal stopping time given by the
[Snell envelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snell_envelope) — a smooth-pasting
condition at the threshold separating "continue" from "stop."

The threshold-policy spec reads as: "prestige when post-reset growth rate ×
multiplier exceeds pre-reset rate by factor $X$," with $X$ computable from the
rate function and discount. Antimatter Dimensions ships a concrete instance —
the Big Crunch autobuyer's `xHighest` ratio mode triggers when the current
run's IP exceeds $X$ times the previous run's best:

<Cite
  repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode"
  path="src/core/autobuyers/big-crunch-autobuyer.js"
  line="115-120"
/>

The threshold formula is concrete; whether it is provably optimal under the IP
geometric-compression growth curve is unproven. That gap is what the bucket
covers.

A stylised reset-cycle model with exponential within-run growth and prestige
reward $C^\beta$ has long-run rate $\rho(u) = r e^{\beta u}/u$, peaked at the
closed-form optimum $u^* = 1/\beta$. The curve does the teaching the algebra
can't — shallow on the early-reset side, steep on the late-reset side, and
the slope of each side tells the player how forgiving their threshold rule
needs to be.

**Discriminators.**

- _vs. combinatorial selection_ — is the decision a time on a process, or a
  configuration? A time is here.
- _vs. discrete planning / scheduling_ — one scalar decision, when, is here. A
  sequence of distinct actions is planning.

## Combinatorial selection

When the player commits a subset or arrangement from a discrete option space —
an upgrade tree, a loadout, a slot fill, a spatial arrangement. Per fixed
selection the system closes: modifier chains collapse to constant multipliers.
Across selections the optimization is a
[combinatorial optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_optimization)
or constraint-satisfaction problem on the option space.

Greedy is sometimes exact. When the feasible sets form a
[matroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matroid), picking by marginal value is
optimal. Prerequisites, mutual exclusions, and budgets break that structure, and
greedy by ratio stops matching the optimum — typically in budget windows where a
cheap node sits behind an expensive prerequisite. The named adjacent math is
knapsack with precedence constraints,
[integer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_programming), and
search with pruning:
[branch-and-bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branch_and_bound) and
constraint propagation. The spec is the option space and its constraints —
nodes, prerequisites, exclusions, budget, per-node effect — and its design claim
is about the frontier: which selections are dominated, which are optimal at
which budget, and where greedy diverges.

Antimatter Dimensions' Time Studies are the clean exemplar. The studies form a
prerequisite tree whose paths exclude each other, the budget is Time Theorems,
and a respec refunds every study — so the tree is a set the player commits and
revises, with no order dynamics:

<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/time-studies/normal-time-study.js" line="64-72" />
<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/time-studies/time-studies.js" line="139-155" />

An eight-node prereq DAG with per-node cost-and-effect makes the
algebra-bifurcation visible: per fixed selection, cost and effect each sum
trivially; across selections, the optimum is a search over $2^N$ subsets.
The greedy by-ratio rule doesn't always match the exhaustive optimum —
budget windows where the cheap-but-prereq-blocked path traps the heuristic
are exactly the contour the search has to track.

**Discriminators.**

- _vs. discrete planning / scheduling_ — does an option's availability or effect
  read state that an earlier option in the same commitment wrote? No, and it's
  here. Order and repeat counts that matter are planning.
- _vs. optimal stopping_ — a configuration, not a time.
- _vs. bilevel interdiction_ — evaluating a selection is a formula, with no
  follower's optimization inside.

## Discrete planning / scheduling

When the player authors an ordered sequence of actions, and each action's
preconditions and effects act on state that later actions read — a queue formed
under finite slots, a build order under capacity, a route walked under a time
budget. Per fixed plan, execution is deterministic and its cost and duration sum
per the algebra. Across plans, the space is sequences with repeat counts, not
subsets, and preconditions make a plan's validity depend on what came before it.
Resource-constrained scheduling is NP-hard, and a closed form for the best
plan's value is rare.

The named adjacent math is
[automated planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_planning_and_scheduling)
— actions as precondition-and-effect schemas over state, in the
[STRIPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Research_Institute_Problem_Solver)
tradition — together with
[job-shop scheduling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job-shop_scheduling) and
state-space search: [A\*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A*_search_algorithm)
when an admissible heuristic is cheap, dynamic programming when the states
decompose, [Monte Carlo tree search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_tree_search)
when branching defeats enumeration but rollouts are cheap, beam search when a
good-enough plan is useful. Special structure closes outright: single-machine
sequencing yields to an exchange argument. The spec is the action schema —
preconditions, effects, cost and duration per action — plus the budget and the
objective. "It's discrete and combinatorial, so there's no math" is the dodge
this bucket exists to refuse.

Idle Loops is the clearest exemplar. Each loop's actions read and write
loop-local state: Haggle spends reputation to lower the price of supplies, Buy
Supplies requires gold at that price, and Buy Mana converts all gold to mana.

- _vs. combinatorial selection_ — does an action's availability or effect read
  state an earlier action in the plan wrote? Yes, and it's here.
- _vs. strategy-space integration_ — no other agent chooses; the rules are
  fixed.
- _vs. stochastic load-bearing_ — the design intent sits in the order the player
  chooses, not in an outcome distribution.
- _vs. optimal stopping_ — a sequence of distinct actions, not one decision
  about when.

## What stays mathematical

Each bucket above gives a *named alternative math* with its own catalog of
theorems and methods. The spec layer doesn't dissolve at the bifurcation; it
changes language. A spec that reads "best-response equilibrium with replicator
dynamics," or "phase-type distribution with mean $\mu$ and CV $\sigma/\mu$," or
"min-cut throughput on a graph with capacities $c_{ij}$" is still precise,
still durable, still the right thing to write down before tuning.

The failure mode this page exists to prevent isn't "the math runs out" — it's
"sim takes over." Throwing a [Monte Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method)
at a stochastic subsystem is calibration, not spec; the spec is the
distribution family. Throwing brute-force tree search at an adversarial
subsystem is calibration; the spec is the equilibrium concept. The distinction
matters for the same reason it matters in the closed-form regime: the spec
compresses the design space and tells you which knobs are independent.

The catalog stops where the algebra stops, and the eight rows above are the
boundary the design space turns at.
