# Power-law shapes

The power-law family is the rate functions of shape $r(x) = a \cdot x^p$ and its close
relatives — constant ($p = 0$), linear ($p = 1$), polynomial ($p > 1$ or $p < 0$), and the
hyperbolic / inverse-power form $r(x) = a / (b + x)^p$. Together they cover almost
every "rate is some power of state" pattern in the [corpus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_law).
What changes from entry to entry is the exponent: positive integers give power-law
acceleration, fractional positive give the fractional-power soft-cap family, negatives give
diminishing returns, and the hyperbolic offset gives a finite-ceiling shape when the
exponent passes one.

This page walks the family in order of increasing exponent variety: constant, linear,
polynomial, then rational. Each entry pairs the rate $r(x)$ with its
[cumulative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiderivative) $R(x) = \int_0^x r(s)\,ds$ and
the [first-passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-hitting-time_model) inverse
$T(C)$ — the time (or level) at which the cumulative crosses threshold $C$.

## Constant rate

$$r(x) = c, \quad R(x) = c \cdot x, \quad T(C) = C / c$$

The trivial baseline. Linear time per unit resource; no acceleration. This is what every
game's level-1 production formula reduces to: a fixed flow, integrated against time, gives
a linear cumulative, and the inverse is constant time per unit. It's also the limit case
that anchors all the others: any rate function that flattens past a soft cap is becoming
constant in that regime, and the cumulative there is asymptotically linear.

There's no demo for the constant case — three flat or linear panels would teach nothing the
formulas don't already say. The shape matters as a reference point against which the
others are read: a non-constant rate is interesting in a way that a constant one isn't, and
the curvature of $R$ and the shape of $T$ are what the catalog is actually about.

## Linear rate

$$r(x) = a + b \cdot x, \quad R(x) = a \cdot x + \tfrac{b}{2} x^2, \quad T(C) = \frac{-a + \sqrt{a^2 + 2bC}}{b}$$

The simplest rate where the cumulative is interesting. Production grows linearly with
state, the integral picks up a quadratic term, and the first-passage time follows the
[positive root of a quadratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_formula). Two design
knobs: $a$ is the rate at $x = 0$ (the floor that makes early progress possible), and $b$
is the slope (how fast production accelerates as $x$ grows). The first-passage scales as
$\sqrt{C}$ when the linear term dominates the constant, and as $C/a$ when the constant
term dominates — early-game and late-game read off the same formula.

Where this shape lives in the corpus: it's the reading of any production formula where
"rate is proportional to count owned." Antimatter Dimensions' production-per-second is
exactly this — `amount × multiplier × tickspeed`, linear in `amount`:

<Cite
  repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode"
  path="src/core/dimensions/antimatter-dimension.js"
  line="588"
/>

```js
let production = amount.times(this.multiplier).times(Tickspeed.perSecond);
```

Pecorella's Part I production line is the same formula in the simpler case where
multiplier and tickspeed are constant — total production at building count $n$ is
$n \cdot r$, linear in $n$. Where the linear rate gets interesting in the modern corpus is
the AD-style dimension cascade: each tier's count grows as the next tier produces it, so
the rate of tier-$k$ accumulation is linear in tier-$(k+1)$ count, which is itself linear
in tier-$(k+2)$ count, and so on. That's the polynomial cascade Pecorella's Part II derives
from the same single linear primitive applied at every layer.

Pure linear rates ($b = 0$) underpace as a primary cost driver — most games reach for
exponentials to get the seesaw — but linear shows up everywhere as a building block:
inside sums, as the early-game regime of a curve that bends later, as the asymptotic
behavior past a soft cap, and as the per-tier rule that compounds into Part II's
$t^k / k!$ shape when iterated.

The first-passage panel makes the $\sqrt{C}$ asymptotic visible: as the threshold grows,
$T(C)$ keeps climbing but at a decreasing rate, because the cumulative is quadratic. This
is the basic reason a "linear rate of growth" feels like progress: the integral grows
faster than the rate, so the milestones come at slowing real-time intervals.

## Polynomial rate

$$r(x) = a \cdot x^p, \quad R(x) = \frac{a \cdot x^{p+1}}{p+1}, \quad T(C) = \left(\frac{(p+1) C}{a}\right)^{1/(p+1)}$$

Generalize the linear rate to an arbitrary exponent. $p = 1$ recovers linear with zero
intercept, $p = 0$ is the constant rate, $p > 1$ is power-law acceleration, and
$p \in (-1, 0)$ is diminishing returns — the rate itself decreases in $x$, but the
cumulative still grows, just sublinearly. The boundary case $p = -1$ integrates to
$a \cdot \ln x$ instead of a polynomial; that's a [logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithm)
in disguise and lives on the [log-frontier page](/rate-functions/log-frontier).

The corpus exemplar is Cookie Clicker's prestige formula, which is a polynomial
_transform_ — Heavenly Chips earned at cookie count $c$ is the cube root of $c / 10^{12}$:

<Cite repo="cookie-clicker" path="main.js" line="3970-3975" />

```js
Game.HCfactor = 3;
Game.HowMuchPrestige = function (cookies) {
  return Math.pow(cookies / 1000000000000, 1 / Game.HCfactor);
};
```

Read as a first-passage: chips $H$ at cookies $C$ is $H = (C / 10^{12})^{1/3}$, which
inverts to $C = 10^{12} \cdot H^3$ — cookies needed to reach chip level $H$ scales as $H^3$.
The chip-as-output side is the polynomial-rate inverse with $p = -2/3$ (a diminishing-returns
rate, since each extra cookie contributes less to chips than the previous). Same shape, two
readings; Cookie Clicker's is the headline cite because it puts the polynomial directly on
the prestige conversion. Faction and ability bonuses across the genre use the same family with various
exponents — $p = 0.5$, the square root, is the default diminishing-returns shape —
each tuned for how aggressively the contribution should diminish.

The shape is fundamental enough that the genre's writeup-level shorthand "sqrt softcap"
collapses the whole family of fractional-positive exponents into one name. The
[softcaps page](/modern/softcaps) treats the design pattern; the math underneath is exactly
this entry's polynomial $r(x) = a \cdot x^p$ for $p \in (0, 1)$.

A log-compressed conversion is the same shape wearing a different formula:
$10^{\log_{10} x / d} = x^{1/d}$, a power law with exponent $1/d$. Prestige-layer currencies
are the usual carrier. Antimatter Dimensions converts antimatter to Infinity Points this
way, and an achievement and a Time Study each lower the divisor $d$:

<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/game.js" line="89-103" />

An upgrade that shrinks $d$ is an _exponent knob_. The shape stays in this entry, but the
upgrade doesn't rescale the threshold the way a
[modifier-product](/rate-functions/composition#modifier-product-state-independent-multipliers)
does — the payout moves by a power of $x$, not by a constant factor, so the same upgrade is
worth more the further the run goes.

<PolynomialRateDemo client:visible />

What the demo makes visible: the panel for $T(C)$ flips orientation between regimes. With
$p > 0$, first-passage is concave — milestones come faster than the threshold suggests. With
$p \in (-1, 0)$, first-passage is convex — milestones come slower as the threshold grows,
because the rate is falling. The crossover at $p = 0$ is the constant-rate baseline where
$T$ is linear in $C$.

## Rational / hyperbolic rate

$$r(x) = \frac{a}{(b + x)^p}, \quad R(x) = \frac{a}{1 - p}\Big[(b+x)^{1-p} - b^{1-p}\Big] \;\;(p \neq 1)$$

The natural way to express "rate falls as state grows" with an explicit floor. The offset
$b$ keeps the rate finite at $x = 0$ (avoiding the singular polynomial case), and the
exponent $p$ controls how fast the rate falls. Three regimes:

- $p < 1$: cumulative is unbounded; sublinear growth. Soft taper without ceiling.
- $p = 1$: cumulative is logarithmic — $R(x) = a \cdot \ln((b+x)/b)$ — and the
  [first-passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_function) is the inverse,
  $T(C) = b \cdot (e^{C/a} - 1)$. The boundary between unbounded and bounded.
- $p > 1$: **cumulative is bounded**, with ceiling $R(\infty) = a \cdot b^{1-p} / (p - 1)$.
  Past that ceiling, no amount of $x$ produces more $R$. The first-passage diverges as
  $C$ approaches the ceiling — there is a finite $x$ for every reachable $C$, but
  unreachable thresholds are real.

The bounded regime ($p > 1$) is the math behind any soft cap with a real ceiling. The
[inverse-square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law) ($p = 2$) is the
canonical case. Civ Clicker's cleric-efficiency multiplier sits in the same family, written
as a saturation rather than a rate:

<Cite repo="civ-clicker" path="scripts/civclicker.js" line="2400-2401" />

```js
1 - 100 / (graveyard + 100);
// graveyard = 0    → 0
// graveyard = 100  → 0.5
// graveyard = ∞    → 1
```

That's $1 - b/(b + x)$ with $b = 100$, which expands to $x/(x + b)$ — a [Michaelis-Menten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michaelis%E2%80%93Menten_kinetics)-style
saturation curve. Reading it as a cumulative: the _increment_ from one extra graveyard is
$d/dx[x/(x+b)] = b/(x+b)^2$, which is the rational rate with $p = 2$. The bounded ceiling
is $1$ — efficiency saturates at 100%, never crosses it. Same math, different framing:
"rate of marginal contribution" versus "cumulative efficiency."

Many gacha-style "next pull is harder" mechanics share the shape: the marginal value of
each pull falls hyperbolically with the count of pulls, the cumulative climbs toward an
asymptote, and the design knob is where to set the ceiling.

<RationalRateDemo client:visible />

Toggle $p$ across 1 to see the regime change. Below $p = 1$ the cumulative panel keeps
climbing; at exactly $p = 1$ it climbs as $\ln$; above $p = 1$ it visibly approaches a
horizontal asymptote and the first-passage panel pinches against an unreachable threshold.
The exponent is the cleanest single design knob in the catalog: one number says whether
the resource is unbounded, asymptotically log, or bounded with a real ceiling.

## What this family is for

The power-law family is the genre's cost and production substrate. Linear, polynomial, and
rational shapes compose with each other under sums and constant multipliers, and each
keeps its closed-form first-passage. They're what designers reach for when they want the
_shape_ of progression to be smooth and the _speed_ to be parameterized by one or two
knobs that read off as derivatives, asymptotes, or ceilings.

What this family doesn't do: rapid acceleration. A polynomial cumulative grows as
$x^{p+1}$, which is fast but never as fast as exponentials. The genre's headline pacing
patterns — geometric cost, exponential production, doubling-time milestones — sit in a
different family entirely. That's the [exponential page](/rate-functions/exponential).
