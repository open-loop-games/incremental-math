# Log and the Lambert-W frontier

This page covers the boundary of elementary closed-form. The
[logarithmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithm) rate has a clean cumulative but its
first-passage requires a non-elementary function — the
[Lambert W function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambert_W_function), defined by
$W(x) \cdot e^{W(x)} = x$. W is closed-form in the formal sense; it has known
[asymptotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_analysis), it evaluates fast, it
composes with the rest of the algebra. But it isn't part of the elementary toolkit most
designers carry, and its appearance in a first-passage formula is the sign that the
underlying spec is sitting on a quiet boundary the catalog should flag.

## Logarithmic rate

$$r(x) = a \cdot \ln(b \cdot x), \quad R(x) = a\,(x \ln(b x) - x + 1/b)$$

The cumulative is what's known as the [$x \ln x$
form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling%27s_approximation) — the same shape that
appears in [entropy](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_(information_theory)>),
combinatorics, and the
factorial's asymptotic expansion. The constant of integration is anchored so $R(1/b) = 0$
(the rate is zero at $x = 1/b$, so the cumulative below that is empty).

The first-passage isn't elementary. Inverting $R(t) = C$ produces an equation of the form
$u \cdot \ln u = K$, which is the prototype Lambert-W equation. The closed form is

$$T(C) = \frac{1}{b} \cdot e \cdot e^{W_0\!\left(\frac{bC/a - 1}{e}\right)}$$

— a substitution into the principal branch $W_0$. The demo evaluates this via
[Halley's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halley%27s_method), which converges in
four or five iterations from a coarse seed.

## Where logarithmic rate appears in the corpus

Pure logarithmic rate — $r(x) = a \ln(b \cdot x)$ as the production rate of a primary
quantity — is rare. What's common is logarithmic _cumulative_ applied as a soft cap on a
quantity that's already accumulated. The two readings often produce the same shape on the
output side, but the role in the system is different: rate-shaped log lives in the
production chain, cumulative-shaped log is a transformation applied at the boundary.

The cleanest cumulative-shaped log in the corpus is Trimps' fluffy pet level, which is
log-base-4 of an underlying XP cumulative:

<Cite repo="Trimps" path="main.js" line="15100-15112" />

```js
var scruffXP = game.global.fluffyExp2 / 1000;
var scruffLvl = Math.log(scruffXP) / Math.log(4);
// ...
var fluffXP = game.global.fluffyExp / (1000 * Math.pow(5, fluffyPrestigeCount));
var fluffLvl = Math.log(fluffXP) / Math.log(4);
```

Each quadrupling of XP adds one level — a fixed $\log_4$ compression of the cumulative.
Reading this as a rate-function entry: the _rate of level gain per XP_ is
$1/(\text{XP} \cdot \ln 4)$, which is the [polynomial rate](./power-law) with $p = -1$ —
the boundary case of the polynomial entry where the integral becomes $\ln$. So Trimps'
log-pet-level is sitting on the same boundary this page covers, just from the other side:
"polynomial rate at $p = -1$" and "log on the cumulative" are two views of the same shape.

## The Lambert W function

W is the inverse of $x \cdot e^x$. Like the
[exponential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_function), logarithm,
and [trig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_functions) families, it's a named
function with well-defined real and complex branches; on the real positive axis the
principal branch $W_0$ is real-valued and monotone. The asymptotic for large $x$ is

$$W_0(x) \sim \ln x - \ln \ln x + \frac{\ln \ln x}{\ln x}$$

— a logarithm of a logarithm, slow-growing past the regime of interest. For practical
evaluation, [Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_method) or
Halley iteration converges to machine precision in a handful of steps.

W appears whenever a closed form mixes exponential and linear in the same equation:

- $a \cdot e^{bx} + cx = d$ — solving for $x$ produces a $W$ of a combination of
  $a, b, c, d$.
- $x \cdot \ln x = c$ — the inverse of the logarithmic-cumulative entry above.
- $x^a \cdot e^{bx} = c$ — closes via $W$ when $a = 1$.

The function was studied by [Lambert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Heinrich_Lambert)
(1758) and [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler) (1779), but its
[formalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambert_W_function#History) as a named
function with stable name and notation traces to a 1996 paper by Corless, Gonnet, Hare,
Jeffrey, and Knuth. For the catalog's purposes it's the natural endpoint of "elementary
closed-form": a result expressible in $W$ is closed-form in the formal sense, but reading
the formula like a designer reads a doubling time requires an extra translation step.

## Practical implications

A few things follow from W being the boundary rather than a wall:

- **A spec resolving to $W$ is still a closed-form spec.** The algebra is intact; the
  formula is exact; numerical evaluation is fast. It's just that the formula doesn't yield
  to the eye the way $T = (\ln 2)/b$ does.
- **W appearing in derivation is a flag.** When two compatible-but-not-identical shapes
  mix in the same equation — log and linear, polynomial and exponential — W is what shows
  up. The design knob is the mixing ratio of the two shapes, and the spec layer can
  describe that knob even if the resulting first-passage isn't a one-line expression.
- **W loses interpretive transparency.** Designers can read $T = (1/b) \cdot \ln(1 + bC/a)$
  and grasp "doubling time scales with $b$." They can't read $T = (e/b) \cdot e^{W(\dots)}$
  the same way. The catalog's pragmatic recommendation: when W appears, calibrate the
  knobs against an oracle (a small evaluator, a calibration table) rather than against
  the closed form directly.

## Spec writeups beyond W

Past W there's a family of named functions that handle further boundaries —
[incomplete gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incomplete_gamma_function),
[exponential integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_integral), [polylogarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polylogarithm) —
each of which arises when the integrand mixes more shapes than W's product of $x$ and
$e^x$ can capture. The catalog stops at W because it's where the genre's actual specs
stop. Past W, the rate-function is in a regime that ships in a research artifact, not in
an idle game.

That said: an absence on the catalog's curve doesn't mean "impossible to write." A
designer who _wants_ the shape of a polylogarithm can write it down; the spec is still
math. The catalog just stops promising one-line readability past W.
