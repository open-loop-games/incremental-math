# Bounded growth

A single entry — the [logistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logistic_function) — but
it's the only [sigmoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmoid_function) in the catalog
whose first-passage is fully elementary, and that uniqueness earns it a page. The shape is
exponential growth limited by an explicit ceiling; the math is one of the older closed forms
in the catalog (due to [Verhulst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Fran%C3%A7ois_Verhulst)
in 1838 for population modeling); and the design intent — "growth that asymptotes to a
real limit, with a visible mid-curve inflection" — is one of the cleanest single-shape
specifications a designer can write.

## The math

The rate is defined on the _state_, not on time: the rate of change depends on how close
the state is to a carrying capacity $K$.

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = r \cdot x \cdot \left(1 - \frac{x}{K}\right)$$

Two factors. The first, $r \cdot x$, is exponential growth — the rate is proportional to the
current state, so the system would double on a constant schedule if left alone. The second,
$(1 - x/K)$, is a brake that turns on as $x$ approaches $K$ — the rate falls toward zero
as the state saturates. Their product is a parabola in $x$, peaked at $x = K/2$ with
maximum $r \cdot K / 4$.

The closed-form trajectory and first-passage:

$$x(t) = \frac{K}{1 + \left(\frac{K - x_0}{x_0}\right) e^{-rt}}, \quad T(C) = \frac{1}{r} \ln\!\left(\frac{K - x_0}{x_0} \cdot \frac{C}{K - C}\right) \text{ for } 0 < C < K$$

The trajectory is the [logistic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logistic_function)
proper — sigmoid in $t$, with the inflection point at $x = K/2$. The first-passage is the
[logit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logit) of $C/K$ adjusted by $\ln((K-x_0)/x_0)$;
elementary, well-defined for any reachable threshold $C \in (0, K)$, divergent as $C \to K$.

The carrying capacity is approached but never reached. This is the entry's distinguishing
feature versus the [rational entry's $p > 1$ case](./power-law#rational--hyperbolic-rate),
which also has a finite ceiling: the rational form's ceiling is reached _in the limit of
infinite $x$_; the logistic's ceiling is reached _in the limit of infinite $t$_. The
ceiling lives in different units in the two shapes.

## What this shape is for

The logistic is the natural specification for any progression value that has a
_meaningful_ maximum — efficiency at saturation, a [population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_dynamics)
constrained by resources, a fraction that should approach but not exceed 100%. In all
three cases the design intent is "growth that knows where it's going," and the logistic
trajectory carries that intent to completion: early, growth is essentially exponential;
mid-curve, the inflection marks the regime change from acceleration to deceleration;
late, the curve flattens visibly toward the ceiling without a hard discontinuity.

The mid-curve inflection is the design feature most often singled out. A linear cumulative
of an exponential rate (the [exponential family](./exponential)) feels uniformly fast; a
sublinear cumulative of a polynomial rate (the [power-law family](./power-law) past
$p < 0$) feels uniformly slow. The logistic feels like _both in sequence_ — the trajectory
shows acceleration through the inflection, then deceleration past it. Players experience a
visible break in the curve's character even though the underlying rate function is one
smooth analytic shape.

## Why pure logistic is rare in the corpus

Most "logistic-feeling" curves in shipped incrementals are not actually logistic. They're
hyperbolic — the [rational entry's](./power-law#rational--hyperbolic-rate)
$1 - b/(b + x)$ shape — because that shape:

- Operates on the _cumulative_ directly rather than on a differential equation.
- Doesn't require a closed-form ODE solution.
- Has the same "approaches a ceiling" qualitative behavior at first glance.

The qualitative behavior past the inflection is genuinely different — the hyperbolic shape
slows monotonically from the start, with no inflection point — but in the regimes most
games operate in, the player can't distinguish them from feel alone, and the hyperbolic
form is cheaper to compute on `Decimal` quantities. So the genre's "soft cap with a
ceiling" is overwhelmingly hyperbolic, while the design _intent_ is often closer to
logistic.

The places pure logistic does show up:

- **Theory-grinder games** that expose the math itself as the puzzle. Exponential Idle's
  late theories are explicit calculus exercises; the logistic shape becomes a teaching
  surface rather than a pacing tool. The shape is not hidden — it's the headline.
- **Population-style mechanics** where the state is literally a population (kingdoms,
  ecosystems, civilizations) and the carrying-capacity framing is part of the fiction. The
  Kittens Game's kitten cap behaves logistically in its design intent; the shipped formula
  uses a piecewise rule but the underlying model the designers built it around is
  carrying-capacity-shaped.
- **Reachability gates** where a stat must approach but not exceed a hard limit (luck
  caps, multiplicative ceilings). When the cap is _narrative_ — "a statistic that
  represents a fraction" — the logistic is the right intent.

For most other "soft cap" use cases, the [hyperbolic entry on the power-law page](./power-law#rational--hyperbolic-rate)
is what shipped games actually compute. The logistic earns its catalog page on
[mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics) grounds, not on prevalence: it
remains the cleanest sigmoid the catalog can offer, and its first-passage being elementary
is what the algebra cares about.

## What logistic doesn't compose with cleanly

Two logistics with shared resource — the
[Lotka-Volterra two-species predator-prey system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka%E2%80%93Volterra_equations)
and its variants — close in special cases (linear couplings, conserved quantities) but in
general are nonlinear coupled ODEs that don't admit a single closed-form trajectory. That's
the boundary between this entry and the bifurcation rows: a single logistic is in the
algebra; coupled logistics generally aren't. Game designs that want "two competing
populations with carrying capacity" are reaching for math that doesn't close, which is why
those mechanics tend to be either simulated discretely or simplified to one of the
linearized special cases.

A logistic with a _time-varying_ carrying capacity ($K = K(t)$ rather than constant) does
admit closed forms in some special cases (linear $K$, exponential $K$) but not in general.
A logistic feeding into another logistic via a multiplier is closed-form when the
multiplier is constant on the integration scale and not when it isn't. The catalog's claim
about composition closure (covered on the [composition page](./composition)) holds for the
logistic the same way it holds for the others: clean compositions stay closed; coupled
compositions migrate into [bifurcations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifurcation_theory).
