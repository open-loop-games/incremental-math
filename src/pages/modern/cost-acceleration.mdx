# Cost Acceleration

Pecorella's Part I assumes the cost line is a single straight slope on a log chart — `cost = base × mult^owned` — and the production line crosses it at a constant rate. The seesaw is set up to tilt forever at the same angle. Whatever the player buys, the next thing costs `mult` times more, and that ratio is locked at the start.

Antimatter Dimensions, [released the same week Pecorella published](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter_Dimensions), shipped with a different shape. For a long stretch of the early game, costs grew exactly as Pecorella described. Then, at a threshold, the slope itself started getting steeper. The eighth dimension at 7,000 purchases didn't cost `mult^7000` of the first one; it cost something far past that, with the multiplier compounding on itself purchase by purchase. The genre called the result the *infinity wall*, and within a few years it had become the default for any game whose late-game design pushed past `1e308`.

This page is about that shape. What it is, why it appeared, how it inverts in closed form, and what it bought designers that pure exponentials couldn't. The underlying algebra — geometric spliced into a faster geometric at a threshold — is the [piecewise splice rule](/rate-functions/composition#piecewise-splice) over two [exponential-family](/rate-functions/exponential) regimes; this page walks AD's design choice, the [composition page](/rate-functions/composition#piecewise-splice) walks the math.

## Pecorella's curve as a baseline

The Part I curve is one parameter family:

$$
\text{cost}(n) = \text{base} \cdot \text{mult}^n
$$

On a log chart, this is a line with slope $\log_{10}(\text{mult})$ and intercept $\log_{10}(\text{base})$. The seesaw — production catches up, you buy, cost jumps, you wait, you buy again — is geometric in time as long as production also scales geometrically. The two lines cross at a constant cadence determined entirely by `mult`.

For teaching purposes this is exactly the right level of abstraction. For a shipping game with a 100-hour late-game, it has one structural problem: the cadence is the only knob. To make late-game purchases feel meaningful, a designer can either crank `mult` from the start (which makes the early game punishing) or layer arbitrary midgame walls on top (which feel arbitrary because they are). Pure exponentials don't *bend*. They scale. A fixed slope is a fixed slope.

The post-Pecorella genre wanted bend.

## What the substrate enabled

Pecorella's framing was implicitly bounded by JavaScript's number ceiling. If the cost line passes `1e308` somewhere in the late game, your variable becomes `Infinity`, comparisons stop working, and the seesaw breaks. Any slope steeper than necessary just hits the wall sooner. The discipline was: pick a `mult` your game can survive for the duration you want, and accept that the *shape* is whatever it is.

The [big-numbers page](/modern/big-numbers) covers what changed in 2018. `break_infinity.js` lifted the ceiling from $10^{308}$ to roughly $10^{1.8 \times 10^{308}}$, and `break_eternity.js` removed it entirely as a practical constraint. The math available to a designer no longer ended at the IEEE 754 cap. With substrate that could carry the values, designers got a free hand to make cost curves whatever shape they wanted them to be.

The first thing many of them wanted was a parabola.

## AD's formula

In log space, AD's cost for the $n$-th purchase past a threshold $T$ is:

$$
\log_{10}(\text{cost}) = n \log_{10}(\text{mult}) + \log_{10}(\text{base}) + \tfrac{1}{2} (n - T)(n - T + 1) \log_{10}(\text{scale})
$$

Below $T$, the third term is zero and the formula collapses to Pecorella's. Above $T$, that third term is a [triangular number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_number) in `excess = n − T`, and it's growing as $O(\text{excess}^2)$ — the *exponent itself* now grows quadratically. The cost as a real number grows exponentially in a quadratic, which is a regime pure exponentials never reach.

The whole calculation runs in log space. AD's actual code is a six-line function:

<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/math.js" line="377-385" />

```js
calculateCost(currentPurchases) {
  const logMult = this._logBaseIncrease;
  const logBase = this._logBaseCost;
  const excess = currentPurchases - this._purchasesBeforeScaling;
  const logCost = excess > 0
    ? currentPurchases * logMult + logBase + 0.5 * excess * (excess + 1) * this._logCostScale
    : currentPurchases * logMult + logBase;
  return DC.E1.pow(logCost);
}
```

Every variable in scope is a plain JS Number. The Decimal materializes only on the final line, when `DC.E1.pow(logCost)` raises 10 to the computed exponent and returns a value the rest of the engine can compare against the player's wallet. As the big-numbers page noted, the substrate's job here is to be a presentation layer for math that doesn't otherwise need it.

The first AD dimension launches with `baseCost = 10`, `mult = 1000`, `scale = 10`, and the threshold pinned at `Number.MAX_VALUE` — which in practice means "wherever the cost first crosses `1e308`":

<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/dimensions/antimatter-dimension.js" line="350-357" />

```js
get costScale() {
  return new ExponentialCostScaling({
    baseCost: ...,
    baseIncrease: ...,
    costScale: Player.dimensionMultDecrease,
    scalingCostThreshold: Number.MAX_VALUE
  });
}
```

The threshold-as-cost-not-purchases choice is its own small design decision: scaling kicks in at the same point where 2016-vintage games would have soft-failed. The infinity wall is positioned to land where infinity used to live.

## Pure vs. accelerating

The two curves on one chart, with sliders for the four parameters that define them. Pure exponential is a straight line on this log scale by construction; the scaling curve coincides with it until the threshold, then bends upward as a parabola in `excess`.

<CostAccelerationCompare client:visible />

A few things to feel for:

**The kink is sharp but not abrupt.** At the threshold, the parabolic term is zero (because `excess = 0`); one purchase past the threshold, it's $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot 2 \cdot \log_{10}(\text{scale})$, which for `scale = 10` adds exactly one decade. Two past, it adds three more decades on top. Three past, six more. The first few purchases past the wall feel like a cliff.

**The pure-exponential slope is irrelevant past the threshold.** Whatever `mult` you set, the parabolic term dominates within ten or twenty purchases of the threshold. The early-game knob and the late-game knob are decoupled — `mult` tunes the warmup, `scale` tunes the wall. Pecorella's single lever became two.

**The threshold is itself a knob.** Drop it from 40 to 10 in the demo and the wall arrives almost immediately; push it to 100 and the curve looks Pecorella-shaped over the whole visible range. The threshold is what lets a designer say "this game has a free-feeling first hour, then the late-game pressure kicks in."

**`scale` is leverage, not addition.** It's a multiplier *on the exponent*. Going from `scale = 2` to `scale = 10` doesn't make the wall ten times higher — it makes it `log10(10) / log10(2) ≈ 3.3` times steeper *as a slope on the log chart*. The wall in real cost is incomparably higher.

## Inverting the parabola

Pecorella's bulk-buy "BONUS CONTENT" depended on the cost curve being invertible: given `money`, what's the largest `n` such that cumulative cost up to `n` is affordable? For pure exponentials the answer is a logarithm. For AD's accelerating curve, you'd think the answer would be intractable.

It isn't. Once you're past the threshold, the equation

$$
\log_{10}(\text{money}) = n \log_{10}(\text{mult}) + \log_{10}(\text{base}) + \tfrac{1}{2} (n - T)(n - T + 1) \log_{10}(\text{scale})
$$

is a quadratic in $n$. Its solution is the [quadratic formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_formula), with coefficients made of pre-computed log constants.

AD precomputes the discriminant and the parabola's center, then solves at every "buy max" call:

<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/math.js" line="367-371" />

```js
updateCostScale() {
  this._precalcDiscriminant = Math.pow((2 * this._logBaseIncrease + this._logCostScale), 2) -
    8 * this._logCostScale * (this._purchasesBeforeScaling * this._logBaseIncrease + this._logBaseCost);
  this._precalcCenter = -this._logBaseIncrease / this._logCostScale + this._purchasesBeforeScaling + 0.5;
}
```

<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/math.js" line="411-415" />

```js
const discrim = this._precalcDiscriminant + 8 * this._logCostScale * logMoney;
if (discrim < 0) return null;
newPurchases = Math.floor(this._precalcCenter + Math.sqrt(discrim) / (2 * this._logCostScale));
```

This is closed-form max-affordable, exact at any scale. The player clicks "Max All" at $10^{500{,}000}$ antimatter and gets the right answer in microseconds, because the inversion is a square root and an addition. There is no loop, no Decimal arithmetic, no upper bound. The substrate carries the *display*; the math runs on doubles operating on logs.

This is the move that made AD's late game playable. A purely numerical max-affordable would either round-trip through Decimal arithmetic on every "buy max" — slow at scale — or bisect over a Decimal range, which is worse. The closed-form inversion of the quadratic-in-log is what lets the wall *feel* like a wall instead of like a freeze.

## Why a parabola in log space?

A parabola in log space — exponential in a quadratic — is one rung above pure exponential on the [hyperoperation hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperoperation). It isn't tetration, but it's the gentlest possible thing that's faster than exponential: it adds polynomial speed to the *exponent* itself.

The reason the genre converged here, rather than on a third or higher level, is mostly inversion. A quadratic-in-log inverts to a square root. A cubic-in-log inverts to a [cubic formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_equation), which exists but is gnarly. A non-polynomial in log inverts to nothing closed-form at all, and "buy max" stops being a constant-time operation. The parabola sits on a Pareto frontier: aggressive enough to be a real wall, simple enough to invert at native speed.

The other consideration is felt-pacing. With pure exponentials, every purchase is roughly the same fraction of the player's bankroll — Pecorella's seesaw at constant tilt. With a parabolic wall, the *first* purchase past the threshold is a small step, the second is a larger step, the third is larger still, and so on. The wall doesn't slap; it tightens. Players describe the late-game AD experience as "watching the next purchase get harder by feel, not by surprise." That experience is the parabola's signature: sublinear acceleration in *purchase number*, which translates to a smooth visual deceleration in the buy rate.

## Other shapes in the wild

AD is the canonical case but not the only one. The post-2016 genre has at least three shapes that fall under "cost accelerates past the early game":

**Variable-exponent (Prestige Tree).** Many Prestige Tree-derived games define their cost as `costMult.pow(costExp)` where both `costMult` and `costExp` are themselves resources that scale with the player's progress. A handful of these games end up with `costExp` that grows logarithmically with the prestige currency, which makes the cost-vs-purchases curve also a parabola in log space — different formula, similar shape:

<Cite repo="Prestige-Tree" path="js/layers.js" line="63" />

The structural difference from AD is that Prestige Tree's curves don't have an explicit threshold. The exponent is always engaged; what changes across the game is which prestige layer is feeding it. AD's threshold model gives a sharp visual kink; Prestige Tree's continuous-exponent model gives a smoother bend. Both are quadratic-in-log over the relevant range, just with different ramp-up shapes.

**Level-dependent multiplier (Synergism).** Synergism's offering quantity formula stacks several exponents on top of each other, with later-game multipliers that turn on past specific thresholds. The composed shape is a piecewise smooth curve where each piece is approximately exponential and each transition is a level-dependent slope-bend. The specific algebra is complicated; the structural answer is the same as AD's. Stack a parabolic term on a linear term in log space, threshold it.

**Dimension-boost-style discrete jumps.** AD itself layers a second mechanism on top of the parabolic scaling: dimension boosts (and later dimension shifts) let the player reset the ladder for a multiplier. This is a different tool — *prestige* in the Pecorella Part III sense — but it interacts with the cost curve. The accelerating cost curve is what makes the prestige *worth* doing; without the wall, the boost wouldn't pay back. The two mechanisms co-evolved.

These are surface variations. The structural template — pure-exponential up to a threshold, polynomial-in-log past it — is what the genre converged on.

## What it didn't fix

**Designer effort.** The threshold and the scaling factor are now two parameters the designer has to tune. AD's first eight dimensions all share the same `costScale` (10) but have different `baseCost`, different `mult`, and an implicit threshold determined by `scalingCostThreshold = Number.MAX_VALUE`. That's seven free parameters per dimension, eight dimensions, and the result is a tuning problem that took years to settle. Pecorella's pure exponential had two parameters per generator and was tunable in an afternoon.

**Curve fatigue.** Once the genre standardized on parabolic acceleration, players started recognizing the shape. A 2020 Reddit discussion about an unfamiliar idle game described the cost curve as "AD-shaped" within an hour of playing. Genre-savvy players read the curve before they read the prose. The wall stopped being a surprise.

**The need for prestige.** Cost acceleration is a tool for slowing the player down inside a single run. It does not, by itself, give the player a way to come back stronger — the seesaw still tilts the same way every reset. The post-Pecorella genre paired cost acceleration with [layered prestige](/modern/layered-prestige) almost immediately; one without the other is incomplete.
