# Skill Curves

Pecorella's progression is wealth: you buy generators, generators make currency,
currency buys more generators. A whole branch of the modern genre encodes
advancement the other way — not as something you _purchase_ but as something you
_accumulate by doing_. A named track gathers experience as the player acts, a
discrete level derives from that accumulator through a curve, and the level drives
a continuous effect on the rest of the game. The curve in the middle is the part
that's actually math, and it's the primary balance lever of every game built this
way.

## The accumulator, the level, the output

The shape is always three stages. First an **accumulator** — a per-track quantity
that only rises, the [integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral) of an XP
rate over the time spent acting, $X(t) = \int_0^t r(\tau)\,d\tau$. Then a
**level**, a discrete number derived from the accumulator by a monotonic curve
$L = \text{xpToLevel}(X)$. Then an **output**: the level drives a multiplier, a
cost divisor, a gate threshold, or a retention bonus that persists through a reset.

Splitting advancement this way gives two things wealth-based progression doesn't.
The accumulator is legible — it only ever goes up, and the player can see exactly
how close the next level is — and the curve is a single, isolated tuning surface.
Reshape the curve and you reshape pacing, softcapping, and compounding all at
once, without touching the actions that feed it.

## XP-to-level curves

Two curve families cover most of the corpus. **Polynomial** leveling sets
$L = \lfloor a\,X^{p} \rfloor$. The common choice $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ is worth
reading carefully: the _level_ is the square root of XP, which means the _XP_ to
reach a level is quadratic in the level. Idle Loops is exactly this — the XP for
level $L$ is $50\,L(L+1)$, and the level read back from the accumulator is its
floored inverse:

<Cite repo="idle-loops-omsi" path="loops/stats.js" line="53-55" note="getExpOfLevel(L) = L·(L+1)·50 — quadratic XP cost per level." />
<Cite repo="idle-loops-omsi" path="loops/stats.js" line="49-51" note="getLevelFromExp floors (√(0.08·exp + 1) − 1)/2 — the square-root inverse, discretized." />

**Exponential** leveling instead sets $L = \lfloor \log_b(X) \rfloor$, so each
level costs a fixed _multiple_ more XP than the last. This is the curve for tracks
that are meant to span many orders of magnitude with a roughly constant
"time-to-next-level" feel under exponential XP production — the same geometric-spacing
trick the [achievements](/modern/achievements) page uses on thresholds.

## The floor makes a staircase

Whatever the curve, the [floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_and_ceiling_functions)
is what makes it feel like leveling rather than a slider creeping upward. The
level is an integer, so a continuous effect $\mu(L) = 1 + cL$ is delivered as a
step function: nothing happens, nothing happens, then the bar fills and the whole
effect jumps at once. The demo plots that delivered staircase against the smooth
curve it discretizes — the gap between them is the "I just levelled up" moment,
and it's why a floored level looks like a reward where a continuous multiplier looks
like wallpaper.

Notice how the level-up thresholds bunch up early and stretch out late under the
polynomial curve: quadratic XP cost means each level is a longer haul than the
last, the sublinear pacing tool the genre chooses when raw exponential production
would otherwise trivialize a track.

## Checkpoints and persistence

Some level-ups do more than nudge a multiplier. **Checkpoint** levels unlock
discrete mechanics — a recipe, a zone, a new action — which act as step shifts in
the rate function rather than smooth bonuses, and show up as sudden accelerations
on a log-log progress plot. Super Turtle Idle gates content behind cumulative kill
counts in exactly this way, each gate a threshold the track has to cross:

<Cite repo="SuperTurtleIdle.github.io" path="logs.js" line="717-724" note="Bestiary gates at cumulative kill counts (10k, 100k) unlock per-enemy bonuses — checkpoint outputs on an accumulator." />

The other design axis is _when the accumulator resets_. In-run skills zero out
each run, so the curve only ever shapes a single run; cross-reset tracks persist
through resets, so retained levels leak power between runs and have to be priced
into the reset economy. The "Groundhog Day" loop games run both modes at once —
an in-run level that drives this run, feeding a permanent accumulator that
sharpens the next one — which is why their balance is so sensitive to the curve:
it's doing double duty.
