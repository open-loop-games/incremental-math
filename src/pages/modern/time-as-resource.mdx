# Time as a Resource

In Pecorella's three parts, `t` is an axis on the chart. Production grows in
`t`. Cost is independent of `t`. Prestige cycles are a way of folding the
`t` axis into discrete laps. Time, in that frame, is the dimension along
which everything else moves; nothing in the math is *about* time.

A wave of games released after about 2017 changed that. Time stopped being
the axis and started being a thing the player owns, allocates, accelerates,
or fights against. The shift is not a single mechanic — it is four distinct
mechanics that all reframe `t` from a coordinate to a quantity.

## Zero-sum time budgets

The cleanest reframe is the time loop. Groundhog Life gives the player a
fixed number of minutes per loop — 1440, the minutes in a day — and a list
of activities competing for them. Whatever you don't spend, you lose at the
end of the loop. Whatever you do spend produces persistent meta-progress
the next loop inherits. The math at allocation time is a continuous
[knapsack problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_knapsack_problem):

$$
\text{maximize} \quad \sum_{i} y_i(m_i) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_i m_i \le T
$$

where `m_i` is minutes given to activity `i` and `y_i` is its yield function.
If yields were linear (`y_i = a_i · m_i`), the optimum would dump everything
into the highest `a_i` and the game would be uninteresting. They aren't.
Time-loop games consistently use *concave* yields — diminishing returns
within a single loop — because the design goal is to make every activity
worth *some* attention, never *all* of it. The minute marginal returns
equalize across activities, you have the optimum.

Omsi-Loops makes the point even more sharply by rewriting the units.
Mana, in Omsi, is time: actions consume mana at a base rate of fifty mana
per second of wall-clock, and each tick of an action increments
`manaUsed` and accumulates `timeSpent`:

<Cite repo="idle-loops-omsi" path="loops/actions.js" line="22-23" />

```js
curAction.manaUsed++;
curAction.timeSpent += 1 / baseManaPerSecond / getActualGameSpeed();
```

A loop has a fixed mana pool. Burning the pool is the loop ending. Mana
*is* the time budget, named differently because the game doesn't want to
admit that the player's optimization problem is "spend 250 minutes
optimally." Cavernous II takes the same shape and makes the queue
explicit: actions go into an ordered list, the list runs deterministically,
the list ends when its summed durations exceed the loop budget. The player
is editing a program whose runtime budget is the loop length.

<Cite repo="Nucaranlaeg.github.io" path="incremental/CavernousII/src/script/main.ts" line="429-439" />

The math is the same in all three. What changes is the player-facing
metaphor — minutes, mana, queued actions — and how the yield curves look.
Concavity arrives by different routes: skill levels saturate, action
prerequisites cap utility, region progress has hard endpoints. The shape is
stable.

<ZeroSumTimeBudget client:visible />

The "apply optimal" button is the closed-form Lagrangian solution for the
square-root yield case, where `m_i* = (a_i² / Σa_j²) · T`. Every actual
time-loop game departs from that closed form because their yield curves are
not pure square roots — they're piecewise functions reflecting unlock gates
and prerequisite skills. But the structural answer is the same: high-`a`
activities deserve proportionally more time; none deserves zero; none
deserves all. The closed form is the floor on solver complexity, not the
ceiling.

What this resource is *not*: spendable currency in the Pecorella sense.
Minutes don't compound. Minutes don't carry over. The loop ends and the
budget refills. The persistent thing is whatever the player produced *with*
the budget — combat XP, faction reputation, a permanent skill point. Time
is the input that keeps replenishing; the output is what stays. This
inverts the Pecorella loop, where the resource (gold, antimatter)
accumulates and gets spent on permanent things called generators that
output more resource. In the time-loop game, the resource resets and the
*generators* — your skill levels, your unlocks — are the persistent layer.

## Speed multipliers as effective time

The second reframe leaves `t` in place but lets the player rescale it.
Antimatter Dimensions ships this as Tickspeed: a purchasable upgrade that
makes ticks *faster*, where production rate is normalized to ticks. Buying
tickspeed once doesn't add a generator; it makes every existing generator
produce on a tighter schedule. The formula gates galaxy-purchasing through
a multiplier on the per-galaxy decay:

<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/tickspeed.js" line="22-67" />

The full function pulls in twelve different effect sources before
returning a `Decimal`, but the structural claim is small: the player has a
running multiplier `s` on game time, and effective elapsed time during a
window is

$$
t_\text{eff}(T) = \int_0^T s(\tau)\, d\tau
$$

— the integral of speed over wall-clock. When `s` is constant, this
collapses to `s · T` and reads as "time runs `s` times faster." When `s`
itself depends on game state — and in AD it depends on galaxies, replicantis,
infinity upgrades, eternity challenges — the integral is genuinely
different from the constant case, because every tick the player buys more
tickspeed and the rate of effective-time accumulation jumps.

Progress Knight 2's Time Warping is a less-mechanically-coupled instance
of the same pattern: a slider that multiplies game speed, unlocked through
the skill tree, peaking at four-figure multipliers.

<Cite repo="progress_knight_2" path="js/main.js" line="567-572" />

The core operation is
identical — `effectiveDelta = realDelta · speedMultiplier` — and so is the
balancing problem. If `s` is bought from production and production scales
exponentially in `t_eff`, then `s` scales exponentially in `t_eff`, and
without intervention the system runs away. The fix is universal: the cost
of `s` itself scales faster than `s`'s payoff, so each new tier of speed
takes longer (in `t_eff`!) than the previous tier delivered. The runaway
becomes a controlled climb where each speed tier delivers a finite amount
of effective time before the next one becomes the obvious purchase.

The mathematical content here is small relative to its impact on the play
experience. Once `s` is unbounded above 1, "spending an hour" becomes
ambiguous. An hour at `s = 100` is a hundred hours of game progression. An
hour at `s = 10000` is past the point where the player can read the
production graph. Mature speed-multiplier games respond by scaling the
*display* — formatted numbers, abbreviated notation, log-axis production
charts — so the player can recognize progress at any speed. The math is
"multiply by `s`"; the engineering is "make the result legible at every
order of magnitude `s` produces."

## Action duration as mutable state

The previous two reframings made `t` a budget and a rescalable clock. The
next move makes the *cost* of each action — its time spend — itself a mutable
quantity that the player reduces over many loops.

Cavernous II and Omsi-Loops both use the pattern. An action's duration is

$$
d_i(\text{state}) = d_i^{\text{base}} \cdot m_i^{\text{special}}(\text{state}) \cdot m_i^{\text{realm}}(\text{state})
$$

where `d_i^base` is reduced by training (each repetition of the action
makes future repetitions slightly cheaper), `m_i^special` is action-specific
modifiers (a relevant equipped item, a prerequisite skill), and `m_i^realm`
is global modifiers (the buff levels, the speed multiplier).

<Cite repo="Nucaranlaeg.github.io" path="incremental/CavernousII/src/script/actions.ts" line="98-106" />

Omsi's Chronomancy does this very directly:

<Cite repo="idle-loops-omsi" path="loops/driver.js" line="13-29" />

```js
function getSpeedMult(zone = curTown) {
    let speedMult = 1;
    if (zone === 0 && getBuffLevel("Ritual") > 0)
        speedMult *= 1 + Math.min(getBuffLevel("Ritual"), 20) / 10;
    // chronomancy
    speedMult *= Math.pow(1 + getSkillLevel("Chronomancy") / 60, 0.25);
    return speedMult;
}
```

The Chronomancy line is exactly a global speed multiplier, but the
buff-zoning above it is the duration-mutation pattern: a Ritual buff makes town-zero actions cheaper
than town-one actions cheaper than town-two actions. The same action queue
runs at different effective costs depending on which zone it's executing
in. The player isn't editing the queue's *shape* between loops; they're
editing the *cost* of the queue's elements through skill investment, and
the same queue therefore fits inside the loop budget that previously
overran it.

Mathematically this is a planning problem under evolving costs. Order of
operations matters because earlier actions in the queue consume budget
that is no longer available for later actions, and earlier actions can
also unlock multipliers (like skill levels) that reduce the duration of
*subsequent* actions in the same loop. A naive solver that treats action
durations as independent constants gets the wrong answer; the real cost
function is `d_i(state_after_actions_1..i-1)`, and the planner is searching
over orderings, not just over inclusion.

The genre's response to this complexity has been "let the player solve it."
Time-loop games typically expose enough of the dependency graph — through
tooltips, action lists with sortable durations, before/after readouts — that
the player can iterate by hand. A few games (notably dmchurch's idle-loops
fork) ship a planner that does the search.

<Cite repo="idle-loops-dmchurch" path="predictor.js" line="344-373" />

The math is real; the engineering choice is whether to surface it.

## Time as pressure inside an active queue

The last reframe gates the existence of `t` on action execution.
Increlution's clock advances only while the queue is running; an empty
or paused queue means no time, no progress, no pressure. The unit being
budgeted is queue-seconds, not wall-clock seconds, and the player gets
zero of them by sitting at the screen with nothing queued.

Inside an active queue, a pressure function grows exponentially in
executed seconds. Increlution's shape is approximately

$$
\text{pressure}(s) = 0.5 \cdot 1.25^{s/60}
$$

— a base of `1.25` per minute of queue runtime. Once pressure crosses
the survival threshold the generation ends and the run resets to its
meta-progression layer; at base configuration this caps a generation
near the hour mark. Skills and equipment in the meta-progression layer
soften the exponent — that is what raises the queue-second budget on
subsequent generations and makes harder action sequences reachable.

The optimization target shifts from "maximize production at `t = ∞`"
to "maximize meta-progress before the threshold trips." That is a
planning problem with a hard deadline measured in queue-seconds: which
sequence of actions, queued in which order, produces the most
permanent skill gain before the survival margin runs out. The genre's
typical late-game framing — accumulate income, idle, return — does not
apply, because the queue runs the same whether the player watches or
not, and the budget is shaped by the queue's contents rather than by
elapsed wall-clock.

Offline progress runs the active queue while the player is away. Time
is yoked to queue execution, not to player presence — set up the queue,
close the tab, and the simulation continues on whatever queue-seconds
remain in the current generation.

A softer version of the pressure idea shows up in mechanics that run on
wall-clock time instead of queue-time: starvation timers in Kittens
Game, seasonal cycles in Evolve, the Ritual buff in Omsi (which decays
between sessions). These push the optimization from "produce" to
"produce *now*" without making `t` itself contingent on queueing
actions. The queue-gated reframe is the strict version of the same
move.

## What the four reframings share

Each disagrees with Pecorella on what `t` *is*, but they agree on a
deeper move: time is no longer a reliable currency the player gets for
free at a uniform rate. The budget version makes it a fixed allotment
that vanishes if unspent. The speed-multiplier version makes it a
quantity the player rescales by buying acceleration. The mutable-duration
version makes it a cost attached to actions, and the cost is itself
optimizable. The queue-gated version makes it contingent on action
execution, with an exponential pressure function that bounds each
generation in queue-seconds.

The mathematical centerpiece in each case is small. Constrained
optimization with concave objectives — closed-form when yields are square
roots, numerical when they aren't. The integral `∫ s(τ) dτ` and the
recurrence problem of `s` depending on game state. Action ordering under
evolving costs. An exponential pressure function bounding a per-generation
queue-second budget. None of these is research-level math. What's
interesting is that Pecorella's original framework has no place for any
of them — and that ten years on, "time as a resource" is one of the
biggest single dividers between games that feel like Cookie Clicker
descendants and games that feel like something else entirely.

The split also reshapes what offline progress means. A time-loop game's
loop budget already represents an active session, so offline simulation
duplicates a mechanic the game tried to make scarce. Speed-multiplier
games can offline-progress as long as they cap accumulated speed.
Queue-gated games run the queue while the player is away rather than
extrapolating production. Only the mutable-duration pattern is fully
compatible with the standard offline model.
