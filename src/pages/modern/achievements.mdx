# Achievements

Pecorella's three parts model production, derivatives, and prestige runs —
things the player _drives_. Achievements are the opposite kind of system: they
sit to the side of the loop and _watch_. The player never acts on an achievement
directly; it fires on its own when the game state it monitors crosses a line. The
mechanic was a garnish in 2016 and is now a load-bearing progression system in most
long-running idle games, sometimes carrying the largest permanent multipliers in
the game.

## Achievements as observers

An achievement is an [observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observer_pattern):
a passive predicate over the game state, evaluated every tick, latching the
first time it reads true. The cleanest way to see this is to read one as code —
the condition is literally a boolean expression over game state:

<Cite repo="derivative-clicker" path="js/data.js" line="85" note="Each achievement is a predicate string over game state, e.g. player.totalManualClicks > 0." />

Two trigger shapes cover almost the entire corpus. A **threshold achievement** fires once a
single tracked quantity passes its threshold — "bake one million cookies" is the
[indicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indicator_function) $\mathbb{1}(S \ge T)$
on the stat $S$. A **collection** fires on a count of other unlocks — "earn fifty
achievements" is $\mathbb{1}(N \ge C)$ where $N$ counts the threshold achievements already
latched. Collection achievements are observers of observers, which is why they
tend to anchor the endgame: they can't complete until the threshold achievements beneath them
have.

## Why threshold achievements feel evenly paced

The interesting math isn't the trigger — it's the _spacing_. Designers almost
never place thresholds linearly (one million, two million, three
million). They place them [geometrically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_progression):
each threshold is a fixed multiple of the last, $T_i = \rho^{\,i}$, usually a
power of ten. The reason is that the watched stat grows exponentially, and a
geometric ladder is the only spacing that turns exponential growth into a steady
drumbeat.

With a stat $S(t) = e^{rt}$ and thresholds $T_i = \rho^{\,i}$, the $i$-th
threshold achievement fires when $e^{rt_i} = \rho^{\,i}$, so

$$t_i = \frac{i \ln \rho}{r} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta t = \frac{\ln \rho}{r}.$$

The gap between unlocks is a constant, independent of $i$. A player twelve orders
of magnitude into the game gets the same achievement rhythm they got in the first
hour, even though the numbers themselves are now incomprehensible. Drag the growth
rate in the demo and watch the threshold lines breathe in and out together — they
stay evenly spaced no matter where the curve goes.

## Coupling rewards back into the loop

A purely cosmetic achievement — a badge, a Steam pop — stops at the observer. The
moment an achievement grants a _multiplier_, it stops being scenery and joins the
production math, and the question becomes how the count couples back in. Two
shapes dominate, and they are the same two the
[modifier-chains](/modern/modifier-chains) page calls "add within, multiply
across".

The **additive** coupling makes the multiplier linear in the count, $\mu = 1 + cN$.
Cookie Clicker's milk is the canonical example: every achievement adds a flat
slice of "milk", and milk feeds each kitten upgrade linearly —

<Cite repo="cookie-clicker" path="main.js" line="5240" note="milkProgress = AchievementsOwned / 25 — milk is exactly linear in the achievement count." />
<Cite repo="cookie-clicker" path="main.js" line="5256-5259" note="Each kitten tier multiplies production by (1 + milk · rate); the upgrades compound as a product." />

— so the milk pool is additive in the count, and the kitten upgrades multiply across
it. The **product** coupling instead multiplies a factor per achievement,
$\mu = \prod_i (1 + c_i)$, which compounds: a game that stacks even a handful of
such rewards reaches multipliers that dwarf the rest of its bonuses.

The demo's reward view plots both against the count. The additive curve is the
cautionary one: because each unlock adds the same $c$, the relative contribution
of the $N$-th achievement is

$$\frac{c}{1 + cN} \xrightarrow{\;N \to \infty\;} 0,$$

so a flat additive reward quietly becomes a rounding error exactly when the player
has earned the most of them. This is the same asymptotic dilution that retires
additive production upgrades — the fix is the same too: make the reward multiply,
or make $c$ itself grow with the achievement's threshold.

## What achievements don't fix

A reward multiplier glued to an observer doesn't create a progression
loop; it decorates one. If the underlying stat has nothing driving it forward, an
achievement that fires at $10^{50}$ just certifies that the player waited. The
observer is honest — it reports what happened — but it can't manufacture a reason
to keep playing. Achievements pace a loop that already works and reward a player
already advancing; they are the genre's most reliable retention system precisely
because they ride on top of progression rather than supplying it.
