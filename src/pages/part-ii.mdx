# Part II — Derivative Growth

Faithful walkthrough of Pecorella's [_The Math of Idle Games, Part II_](https://www.sirpinski.com/the-math-of-idle-games-part-ii/) (originally published October 2016). Part I treated the standard Cookie-Clicker shape — generators each producing primary currency, with exponential cost and linear-with-multipliers production. Part II asks: what if the generators don't produce currency directly, but produce other generators?

## Generators producing generators

In the standard model, every generator pumps into the same pool: AdVenture Capitalist's investments all generate currency, Clicker Heroes' heroes all generate damage. Pecorella's question for Part II: drop that constraint. Wire the generators in a _cascade_ instead.

A single Tier 1 generator produces currency. Tier 2 produces Tier 1. Tier 3 produces Tier 2. And so on. Each tier above the first is the **rate of change** of the tier below it. If you remember calculus, that's a [derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative) — the cascade is a sequence of [integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral) from the top tier down to currency.

The setup matters. With a single Tier 1 generator that produces 1 currency per second:

$$\frac{dC}{dt} = 1, \quad C(t) = t$$

Currency rises linearly. With a single Tier 2 that produces 1 Tier 1 per second, Tier 1 itself rises linearly in $t$, so currency is the integral of that:

$$\frac{dC}{dt} = T_1(t) = t, \quad C(t) = \frac{t^2}{2}$$

That's a parabola. Stack another tier — a Tier 3 producing Tier 2 producing Tier 1 producing currency — and currency follows $t^3 / 6$. The pattern is the [Maclaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series) for $e^t$:

$$1, \; t, \; \frac{t^2}{2}, \; \frac{t^3}{6}, \; \frac{t^4}{24}, \; \dots, \; \frac{t^n}{n!}$$

$$e^t = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!}$$

A finite cascade is a _partial_ Taylor sum of $e^t$. Add infinite tiers and the cascade _becomes_ exponential growth.

The same structure appears in the generators themselves. In a depth-4 cascade seeded by a single Tier 4 generator, the total count of all generators at time $t$ — the three tiers below the seed — is $1 + t + \frac{t^2}{2} + \frac{t^3}{6}$: the same partial sum, shifted one order down from the currency output. The cascade has the Taylor sum at every tier simultaneously.

## The half-existent rule

Before plotting the parabola, Pecorella pauses on a discrete-time wrinkle. If a Tier 1 generator only fully exists at the end of the second it was created, but it's nominally producing currency continuously, what do we count for that partial second?

His rule: a Tier 1 born during second $t$ contributes only $\frac{1}{2}$ currency that second. By the end of the second it's fully real, so the _next_ second it produces a full unit. This is the [trapezoidal rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trapezoidal_rule) by another name — and at depth 2 it puts the discrete simulation exactly on $t^2/2$ at every integer $t$. The half-on-creation rule is unique at depth 2 — there, the tier above (Tier 1) is linear in $t$, and trapezoid integration of a linear function is exact. From depth 3 onward the tier above is quadratic or higher, so the rule loses its exactness and the discrete simulation drifts off the theoretical $t^n/n!$. Pecorella keeps the prose at depth 2 for exactly this reason.

## A continuous cascade

Pecorella's spreadsheet `2a` simulates the cascade with plain [forward Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_method) at $\Delta t = 1$:

$$T_i(t+1) = T_i(t) + T_{i+1}(t) \cdot r_i$$

— the sheet indexes tiers from 0, so its $T_i$ is Tier $i+1$, and $r_i$ is the rate at which one Tier $i+2$ generator produces Tier $i+1$. The sheet defaults: 5 tiers, top-tier seed of 1, base production at Tier 1 of 5/sec, and per-tier rates $r_0 = 0.1$, $r_1 = 10$, $r_2 = 3$, $r_3 = 1$. Currency accumulates as $\int T_0(t) \cdot \text{baseIncome} \, dt$.

The demo below runs that simulation. Drag depth down to 1 and the curve is straight-linear; bump it up to 5 and the cascade shape appears. Rate scale uniformly multiplies all per-tier generation rates — useful for stretching or compressing the timeline without changing the shape.

## Cascade vs. exponential

The interesting payoff: a derivative cascade _almost_ looks exponential, but it's bounded by its degree. Each added tier extends the polynomial degree by one, so each tier's growth catches a slower exponential for a while, then loses to it eventually. The whole point of an exponential is that it eventually catches every polynomial — no matter the depth — but for finite-runtime games, the cascade can stand in for exponential growth perfectly well.

The chart below overlays $t^n/n!$ for depths 1 through 5 against a Cookie-Clicker-style $\text{base} \cdot r^t$ exponential. On linear scale, all the polynomials look like nothing next to the exponential by the time $t$ gets interesting. On log scale (the default), the exponential is a straight line and the polynomials are slow-curving — depth 5 is the highest curve early on, but eventually every polynomial bends below the exponential's straight log-line.

A few patterns the chart makes legible:

- **Higher depth wins early, exponential wins late.** A 5-tier cascade outpaces a 1.07-per-tick exponential for a long stretch, then loses. The crossover depends only on the exponential's growth rate.
- **Cost is still exponential.** The trick of the cascade is that _production_ grows polynomially while costs stay exponential — same seesaw as Part I, just with a slower cascade. So the run still ends naturally; it just takes longer to get there per added tier.
- **Each tier feels qualitatively different.** Pecorella's pitch from the original post: it "feels" like multiple AdVenture Capitalist games stacked. The lower tiers go enormous because they're integrating something that itself was integrating something. _Derivative Clicker_ (gzgreg) is the canonical example.

## Designing past the simplest case

The simplest cascade — one cascade, one currency, no cross-tier upgrades — is what the demos above visualize. Real games rarely ship that pure. _Derivative Clicker_ verifies the Part I seesaw still holds: its High Schoolers cost $5 \times 1.1^n$, still exponential — sub-exponential cascade production against exponential costs, balanced as expected. The harder problem is keeping lower-tier purchases relevant when the cascade is handing you billions of them free per second. gzgreg's solution: each manually purchased generator boosts all same-tier generators by 0.05%, and the UI shows both the free-plus-bought total and the purchased-only count in parentheses — the modifier makes buying by hand feel meaningful at any scale. _Shark Game_ (Cirrial) takes a different angle: Nurse Sharks create Sharks that harvest fish, generators spanning multiple currencies rather than a single cascade. The derivative cascade is a primitive — make generators, draw lines between them, see what happens.
