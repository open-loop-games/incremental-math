# Part I — Exponential Growth & The Seesaw

Faithful walkthrough of Anthony Pecorella's [*The Math of Idle Games, Part I*](https://www.sirpinski.com/the-math-of-idle-games-part-i/) (originally published October 2016). Each chart in the original post is replaced here with an interactive demo wired to the same formulas and parameter ranges Pecorella used. Where Pecorella illustrates a point with a static line, this page renders it as a slider-driven plot so the relationship between the inputs and the curve is the thing you actually play with.

## Terminology

Pecorella opens by pinning down the vocabulary the rest of the math uses.

- **Primary currency.** The number being incremented — usually money. Almost always the long-run goal.
- **Generator.** An item the player buys that produces primary currency. Its rate is in currency per second.
- **Primary exchange currency.** Some games (Clicker Heroes, for instance) have generators produce a step-removed currency — DPS — which is then converted to gold. The exchange step gives the designer an extra knob over how the primary currency curves.
- **Multiplier.** Anything that scales generator production: explicit upgrades, milestone bonuses for owning N of a thing, achievements. Their job is to drag production back up toward the cost line, temporarily.
- **Prestige.** A reset of most of the game state in exchange for prestige currency or persistent multipliers — a "New Game+" pattern for the long run. Prestige is Part III's territory.

## The seesaw

At the simplest level, an idle game is a tug-of-war between two curves. Cost grows exponentially in the number you already own, by a cost ratio that Pecorella's sheet calls `rate_growth`; production grows linearly.

$$\text{cost}_\text{next} = \text{cost}_\text{base} \times (\text{rate}_\text{growth})^\text{owned}$$

$$\text{production}_\text{total} = (\text{production}_\text{base} \times \text{owned}) \times \text{multipliers}$$

Both curves are in the [rate-function catalog](/rate-functions): cost is the
[exponential family](/rate-functions/exponential)'s geometric ladder; production is the
[power-law family](/rate-functions/power-law)'s linear rate. The catalog walks each shape
in isolation — what its cumulative looks like, how its first-passage time scales — which
is the math Pecorella derives by hand below from the seesaw of the two together.

For AdVenture Capitalist's Lemonade Stand, Pecorella uses $\text{rate}_\text{growth} = 1.07$, $\text{cost}_\text{base} = 3.738$ (the first stand is free, so the *second* one is what costs about four currency), and $\text{production}_\text{base} = 1.67/\text{sec}$. With $N = 10$ owned the next stand costs $\approx 7.87$ while production is already at $16.7/\text{sec}$ — well ahead. The whole point is what happens further out.

A few things the chart makes visible that the formulas alone don't:

- **Milestone ×2 bumps.** AdVenture Capitalist (and most games of its lineage) hand out a stacking ×2 multiplier each time the player crosses 25, 50, 100, and 200 owned. Each bump shoves the production line back up under the cost line for a while — this is the "seesaw" actually seesawing. Toggle the bumps off to see the clean linear-vs-exponential race underneath.
- **Log scale changes the story.** On a linear scale, exponentials look like a hockey stick that crushes everything. The seesaw at low owned counts — where most of the *play* happens — is invisible. Switch to log and the linear production line becomes a slow upward curve, the exponential cost line becomes a straight ramp, and the tradeoff between them is legible across many decades of $N$.
- **Why it ends.** Mathematically, $n^x$ for $n > 1$ catches and overtakes any polynomial $x^k$ no matter how small $n$ is or how large $k$ is. The crossover may be far out — that's what balancing tunes — but it's inevitable. Once cost is enough decades above production that a single buy takes prohibitively long, the run is effectively over until the player resets (Part III) or the designer hands out a new generator (next section).
- **The cost ratio is a business model decision as much as a design one.** AdCap's 1.07 and Cookie Clicker's 1.15 were tuned for browser-era play: long sessions, little monetization pressure, runs that felt expansive. Mobile idle pushed the number up. When revenue depends on players hitting walls and paying to bypass them — IAP skips, rewarded ads — a higher cost ratio compresses the loop and creates more monetization events per session; the incentive structure behind the parameter changed, not the math. The later desktop revival on Steam pulled the other way: one-time-purchase games have no reason to manufacture walls, and their players tend toward longer sessions, which rewards the slower end of the range.

## Bulk buy: closed-form instead of iteration

Pecorella tucks this away as **bonus content** at the end of Part I, but it's the most reusable bit of math in the whole post. The cost of buying $n$ more generators starting from $k$ already owned is the sum of a [geometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_series):

$$\text{cost}(n) = \text{base} \times \frac{r^k - r^{k+n}}{1 - r}$$

— which is just $\sum_{i=k}^{k+n-1} \text{base} \cdot r^i$ with the closed-form for that sum substituted in. That collapses an O($n$) iteration into an O(1) expression. Inverting it (set the cost ≤ current currency, solve for $n$) gives the dual question — "how many can I afford right now?" — also in closed form:

$$n_\text{max} = \left\lfloor \log_r\!\left( r^k - \frac{\text{cash} \cdot (1 - r)}{\text{base}} \right) - k \right\rfloor$$

Pecorella derived both by hand. (The discrete-sum closed form has a continuous-integral counterpart that agrees with it to within a few percent at typical incremental ratios — the [Riemann-sum approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_sum) error.) The demo below computes the same answers directly from a single declared cost expression — sliders match Pecorella's spreadsheet defaults (base cost 20, cost ratio 1.05, owned 10, currency 400).

## Multiple generators: which is the best buy?

A real game has more than one generator. Once there are five-plus options with their own base production, base cost, and cost ratio, the player is making an economic decision at every purchase opportunity. Pecorella defines "optimal" with a concrete score per candidate purchase:

$$\text{score} = \frac{\text{nextCost}}{\text{totalIncome}} + \frac{\text{nextCost}}{\text{itemBaseIncome}}$$

Read in seconds: how long until you can afford the next one, plus how long that one needs to run before it pays for itself. Pick the generator with the lowest score. Recompute after every purchase. (Pecorella's score is one criterion among several — plain `cost/income`, `cost/itemIncome`, or [NPV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_present_value) with a discount rate are common alternatives.)

Defaults below are the actual AdVenture Capitalist values for the first five businesses. Drag the currency slider up and watch the "best buy" highlight shift — and also watch how it shifts again as you raise an early generator's owned count and other generators become viable.

Two caveats Pecorella flags before showing his version of this table:

- **The model simplifies production to per-second.** Real generators in AdCap and its descendants run on a timer — buy a thing, wait $T$ seconds, collect. Treating production as continuous-per-second favors slower, higher-end generators that haven't actually paid out yet.
- **Players don't actually play this.** The "best buy at every step" policy is far too micromanage-y for a human — *"buy one Gen 2, then three Gen 1s, then a Gen 3, then another Gen 1..."*. Real players bulk buy or aim at round numbers. The score is a model of theoretical optimum, not a play pattern.

A pattern that pops out as you play with the demo: when *no* milestones are giving anyone a leg up (the default), the **highest-tier generator you can afford is almost always the best buy** as soon as it's affordable. The decision collapses and older generators feel pointless. Milestone bonuses are how Pecorella un-collapses it.

## Milestones reshape the priority order

Pecorella defines two milestone variants in sheets 1d and 1e. Both schedule a multiplier at owned-counts 25, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 1000, and the bonuses **stack** as the player crosses each one — same convention Pecorella calls out for 1a (*"a (stacking) x2 multiplier"*). The sheets label their input column "Bonus (not cumulative)" but that's about the input format; each cell is the per-milestone multiplier, not the running product. The calc sheet then builds the cumulative lookup behind it.

- **Global (sheet 1d).** Every generator shares the same per-milestone multiplier table: ×5, ×5, ×2, ×2, ×3, ×4, ×2, ×3, ×4. Cross all nine and the running bonus is $5 \times 5 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 4 \times 2 \times 3 \times 4 = 28{,}800\times$.
- **Individual (sheet 1e).** Each generator gets its own column. Pecorella's defaults push different generators forward at different points — generator 2 spikes hard at 50 (×10), generator 1 spikes at 100 (×10), generator 3 spikes early at 50 (×8). The priority order shuffles across the run.

The same demo above contains both modes via the radio toggle next to "milestones". Watch the **bonus** column update as you raise an owned count past 25, 50, 100 — each crossing multiplies the column. With **global** the priority shifts but everyone shifts together. With **individual** the next-best-buy genuinely rotates between generators across the run — the decision stays interesting longer because no single generator dominates indefinitely.
