# Composition — when mechanics blend

This page is for designers who've felt the difference between Antimatter Dimensions and a
mechanic-of-the-week clicker — the difference between mechanics that keep mattering as
the game goes and mechanics that become "set-and-forget" the moment the autobuyer
unlocks. Five questions in that area, each one with a math diagnosis you can apply
without spreadsheet calibration.

The whole page rests on one architectural fact about
[composition rules](/rate-functions/composition): mechanics that contribute _additively_
to a rate eventually fall behind anything growing faster — the asymptotic-dominance
ordering on the catalog page is doing this, and nothing in the algebra prevents it.
Mechanics that contribute _multiplicatively_ to a shared rate can't fall behind the same
way; multiplicative composition is independent of magnitude. The choice between these
two architectures is the entire question.

## Why does this mechanic become obsolete once automated?

The honest version: it isn't the automation. The automation just makes the obsolescence
_visible_. The math was always headed there.

When a mechanic is its own [first-passage problem](/rate-functions/) — its own currency,
its own milestone, its own rate function feeding back into nothing the rest of the game
cares about — it lives in isolation from the main loop's rate function. The total
production of the game decomposes as a sum: $r_{\text{total}}(t) = r_1(t) + r_2(t) + \dots$.
Once the player has automated $r_1$, its growth rate is fixed at whatever the autobuyer
produces. Meanwhile, the post-automation upgrade tree pushes some other rate $r_3$ into
exponential growth, and the catalog's [asymptotic ordering](/rate-functions/composition)
takes over. After enough time, the share $r_1 / r_{\text{total}}$ collapses to ~0%. The
mechanic isn't _broken_; it's _dominated_. From the player's perspective, the
indistinguishability of "broken" and "dominated" is what makes the experience feel like a
chore to pretend matters.

The fix isn't more upgrades, more milestones, more decoration. The fix is to change
the composition: stop having the mechanic add to the rate, start having the mechanic
_multiply_ something.

## Why do AD's galaxies feel like they keep mattering?

Same math, opposite direction. AD's galaxies are not an additive contribution to
production — they're a state variable that _modifies_ production. The composition rule
underneath is the [modifier-product](/rate-functions/composition):
$r(x) = r_{\text{base}}(x) \cdot \mu_{\text{galaxies}}$. The galaxy count is a single
shared state that many mechanics feed. Tickspeed boosts feed it. Replicanti unlocks feed
it. Eternity upgrades feed it. Time studies feed it. Every one of those mechanics is
adding to the _same_ state variable, which is then multiplying production downstream.

The architectural difference: galaxies aren't a separate first-passage problem with their
own milestone the player is racing toward. They're a _modifier_ on the main loop's rate
function. Once a mechanic feeds the galaxy count, that mechanic's contribution
permanently boosts everything downstream — not as an additive term that gets dominated
by something faster, but as a multiplicative factor that scales the entire downstream
rate. Multiplicative contributions don't get dominated. They compose.

The visual claim the demo makes: identical growth rates, identical mechanics, two

keep mattering because the math is structurally different — there's no asymptotic
ordering on multiplicative factors, only on additive ones.

The galaxy count itself is the sum of contributions from many sources, and that sum
_does_ have an asymptotic ordering — the fastest-growing source dominates the galaxy
count over time. But the galaxy count is then _multiplied_ into production, and the
multiplication is what blends. A designer worrying about galaxy-source obsolescence
would be one level too low: the relevant unit is the multiplier, not its component
sources, and the multiplier blends into everything that uses galaxies.

## What are my options for boosting a mechanic?

Four canonical levers, each one a different composition rule from the catalog. They're
not interchangeable; each does something specific to the rate function and the
first-passage time.

**Multiplier on the rate.** $r(x) \to \mu \cdot r(x)$. The
[modifier-product](/rate-functions/composition) rule. Rescales the timescale: doubles
the rate, halves the time-to-threshold, identical curve shape on log-y. Every "+50% to X"
upgrade is this. The math is clean: $T_{\text{new}}(C) = T_{\text{old}}(C / \mu)$. Players
feel this as "everything's faster now."

**Lower the threshold.** $C \to C / k$. The cost the player has to reach is rescaled
down. Identical math to the multiplier case from the time perspective —
$T(C / k) = T_{\text{multiplier}}(C)$ — but the framing is different: the _cost line_
moves down rather than the _production line_ moving up. Players feel this as "the next
thing is cheaper now," and the difference matters for which side of the seesaw the
upgrade reads as belonging to.

**Multiply the state amount.** The state variable itself gets rescaled: $x \to k \cdot x$.
On log-axis charts this is a horizontal shift; on linear axes it's a stretch. For
geometric rate ladders, multiplying the state by $k$ means starting $\log_m k$ levels
ahead. Players feel this as "I jumped forward in the curve" — qualitatively different
from speeding it up, because the _position_ on the curve changed.

**New source.** The state is fed by an additional [sum](/rate-functions/composition)
term. The mechanic that produces the state now has another contributor. Crucially: this
is additive only at the _state_ level; the state is then multiplied into rates downstream,
so the new source's contribution still blends multiplicatively into the loop. The trick
of AD's galaxies is exactly this two-level structure: many sources sum into the shared
state, the shared state multiplies the rate.

The four are interchangeable in their effect on first-passage time _for a single milestone_
— each one shortens it — but they're not interchangeable in how they read to the player or
how they compose with each other. Multipliers stack with multipliers; thresholds stack
with thresholds; new sources stack with each other and with everything else. Designing
upgrades means picking which lever each upgrade pulls, and the picks compound into the
texture of the late game.

## How does prestige fit into the loop?

[Functional composition](/rate-functions/composition): the prestige currency $H$ is a
function of the primary cumulative $C$ (often $H \propto C^{1/3}$ or $H \propto \log C$),
and the post-prestige run is the cumulative integrated against a rate function that's
_been modified by_ $H$. Two layers, glued by a function — primary loop closes for one
run, prestige is the conversion at the layer boundary, post-prestige loop runs against a
rate that includes $H$ as a modifier.

The shape this gives the math: as long as each layer is in the catalog and the
prestige conversion is monotone elementary, the whole stack has a closed-form first-passage.
This is what makes modern incrementals tractable — the _layer_ is the unit of math, not
the run, and runs nested arbitrarily deep stay in the algebra as long as the gluing
functions stay simple.

Where prestige stops being closed-form is when the _cycle length_ itself becomes a
decision variable. The question "when should the player prestige?" is an
[optimal-stopping problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_stopping), which lives
in the [bifurcation rows](/rate-functions/composition#what-doesnt-compose-cleanly) of
the catalog. Per-run math closes; cycle-length math doesn't. The right adjacent math
there is dynamic programming or a Bellman-equation solver, not the catalog. Most modern
games avoid this by _not_ leaving the cycle length up to the player — autobuyer-driven
prestige with a fixed trigger, or guided by milestone unlocks, both convert the
optimal-stopping problem into a deterministic schedule that closes again.

## How do I add a new layer without making the old one feel pointless?

The previous four questions all bear on this one. The diagnosis is a single test: does
the new layer's value flow _back into_ the old layer's primitives? If yes, the old
layer's primitives now have a new reason to exist — they feed something new — and the
old layer keeps mattering. If no, the old layer is sitting in additive composition with
the new layer, and the asymptotic-dominance ordering has already decided which one is
load-bearing in the long run.

Three concrete versions of "yes":

- **The new layer's currency is a multiplier on the old layer's rate.** Cookie Clicker's
  Heavenly Chips multiply CPS; you keep upgrading buildings post-prestige because the
  Chips multiplier and the building output are _multiplicatively_ composed.
- **The new layer's currency unlocks new sources for the old layer's state.** A new
  prestige tier opens additional ways to produce the primary currency. The old loops
  matter because the new sources flow through them.
- **The new layer's currency lets you crank a knob on the old layer.** A challenge
  mode rebalances the old loop's parameters. The old loop is what's being challenged;
  the player's knowledge of it is now a real asset.

The version of "no" is the one that produces the obsolete-mechanic feeling: a new layer
whose currency does its own thing, has its own upgrades, and never composes with the old
layer's rate function. This is what the [layered prestige](/modern/layered-prestige) page
calls "loosely-coupled tiers," and it's the loosest of the bad design patterns the
catalog can diagnose: tiers that share a screen but not a rate function are tiers
running their own first-passage problems in the same UI.

## Where the math stops

Two boundaries the catalog can't see across:

- **It doesn't tell you which lever a specific upgrade should pull.** The four canonical
  boosts are all valid; the math says nothing about whether a particular upgrade should
  be a multiplier or a threshold drop or a new source. That's a feel call, and feel
  calls are what designers are paid for. The math just maps the space the call is being
  made in.
- **It doesn't tell you whether to add the new layer at all.** The math diagnoses
  obsolescence; it doesn't prescribe novelty. If a mechanic is going to go obsolete and
  there's no clean way to compose it back into the main loop, _removing_ the mechanic
  is also a valid response, and the math has nothing to say about which is the right
  call. Some games deliberately retire mechanics to make room for new ones; some
  deliberately keep all mechanics composing forever. Both are real design philosophies.

What the catalog _does_ tell you, reliably, is whether your existing composition will
hold up — whether the addition you're contemplating sits in additive position relative
to existing rates (will go obsolete), in multiplicative position (will blend), or in
some other composition the catalog has named. That diagnosis runs faster than tuning
runs do, and it surfaces problems before the playtests would.
