# Modifier chains — stacking that stays legible

The headline rate-function in any modern incremental is the output of a long sequence
of additions, multiplications, and an occasional exponent. The math underneath is the
[modifier-product rule](/rate-functions/composition#modifier-product-state-independent-multipliers) — modifiers on rates are linear in
the cumulative when they're state-independent — but the design call is which kind of
modifier each upgrade is, and how many can stack before the chain becomes illegible.

This page bundles the questions that come up when designing a modifier system:
additive vs multiplicative as the primary distinction, when state-dependence
breaks the algebra, and what the chain's shape does to player legibility.

## When should a modifier be additive vs multiplicative?

The math first. With a base rate $r_0$:

- **Additive modifier**. $r = r_0 + \mu_a$. Increases the rate by a fixed amount. The
  cumulative is shifted by $\mu_a \cdot t$; the first-passage is shortened by an
  amount proportional to $\mu_a / r_0$. The contribution is a _constant share_ of the
  rate at the moment of application — but as $r_0$ grows past $\mu_a$, the additive
  modifier becomes a vanishing share.
- **Multiplicative modifier**. $r = r_0 \cdot \mu_m$. Scales the rate by a constant
  factor. The cumulative is scaled by $\mu_m$; the first-passage threshold rescales
  inversely. The contribution is a _fixed factor_ — independent of how big $r_0$ is.

This is the same math as the [composition page](/design-playbook/composition)'s "obsolete vs blended"
distinction, but applied at the modifier level rather than the mechanic level.
Additive modifiers go obsolete when the base rate outgrows them; multiplicative
modifiers don't.

The design call:

- **Use additive when the modifier's contribution is meaningful at the scale of the
  _current_ rate.** A "+5 per second" upgrade is meaningful when the base is 10/sec
  and useless when it's 10⁶/sec. That's fine if the upgrade is _meant to be_
  early-game-only — it served its role and retired. It's a problem if the upgrade
  was meant to stay relevant.
- **Use multiplicative when the contribution should keep mattering.** A "×2 to
  production" upgrade keeps mattering forever, regardless of how big production
  gets. Every doubling of production keeps the multiplier's contribution in
  proportion.
- **Use additive _into_ a multiplicative state when you want the additive to
  blend.** This is the AD pattern: many sources contribute additively to the galaxy
  count; the galaxy count is the multiplier on production. Each individual source's
  contribution to the galaxies is additive, and the galaxies are multiplicative on
  the rest of the game. The additivity at the source level is what makes
  multi-source flexibility work; the multiplicativity at the consumption level is
  what keeps the sources from going obsolete. The shape lives in one return
  expression — five additive contributors, summed, then handed downstream as the
  tickspeed multiplier:

  <Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/tickspeed.js" line="3-20" />

  ```js
  export function effectiveBaseGalaxies() {
    let replicantiGalaxies = Replicanti.galaxies.bought;
    // ...
    return Math.max(player.galaxies + GalaxyGenerator.galaxies + replicantiGalaxies + freeGalaxies, 0);
  }
  ```

  The two-level structure is the whole pattern: each source is additive into the
  galaxy count (its contribution can be designed in isolation, against the other
  sources without coupling), and the galaxy count itself is multiplicative on
  every dimension's production downstream (which is what keeps each source from
  going obsolete as the run progresses).

The genre default in modifier-chain UIs — visible add-then-multiply pipelines — encodes
exactly this discipline: the additive layer is where mechanics sum into shared state,
the multiplicative layer is where that state acts on production. Putting the
add-and-multiply order _backwards_ (multipliers that just add to other multipliers)
is the failure mode that produces "this whole upgrade tree feels useless past tier 3"
— if the modifiers are stacking additively where they should compose multiplicatively,
the late game is back to the [obsolete-mechanic problem](/design-playbook/composition).

The two curves are the same modifier values composed two different ways. The additive
stack flattens into a single bonus the player can read on a tooltip; the multiplicative
stack compounds into a number that grows with each new source the same way prestige
multipliers grow with each new layer. The architectural choice is which axis the
upgrades stack along, and the gap widens with every additional source.

## When does state-dependence break the algebra?

The catalog's [modifier-product rule](/rate-functions/composition#modifier-product-state-independent-multipliers) closes when
the modifier $\mu$ is _state-independent_ — doesn't depend on the same state $x$ the
rate does. State-dependent modifiers are formally a different rate function:
$r(x) \cdot f(x)$ for some $f$, which doesn't simplify and generally doesn't have an
elementary cumulative.

In design terms: a modifier that grows as the player's _current production_ grows
(rather than, say, as the player's current galaxy count grows) is state-dependent
on the rate it's modifying. The math's diagnosis is that this composition usually
falls outside the algebra. The design-side consequences:

- **First-passage no longer closes.** The math can't tell the player when they'll
  hit the next milestone in closed form; the calculation has to be done numerically.
  Practically this means the game can't ship a max-affordable-buy calculator that
  works in the late game; calibration tables or solvers replace closed-form lookups.
- **Modifier behaviors get weird at scale.** A multiplier that compounds with the
  thing it's multiplying tends toward [super-exponential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetration)
  growth. Past a certain scale, the player's production grows faster than any
  finite-precision big-number library can represent. Games that ship this kind of
  modifier accidentally are usually playing a game of cat-and-mouse with overflow.
- **Designer intent gets cloudy.** The whole point of a multiplier on a rate is to
  _rescale_ the rate's curve. State-dependent multipliers don't rescale; they
  _transform_. The shape of the late-game curve becomes whatever the modifier's
  state-dependence produces, often something the designer didn't specifically intend.

When state-dependence is the right call: when the _transformation_ is the design
goal. Power-modifiers on rates ("triple the exponent of your production formula")
are explicitly state-dependent; they're producing a [polynomial transform](/rate-functions/power-law#polynomial-rate)
of the rate, and the late-game shape that emerges is the shape the designer chose.
The math just has to be acknowledged: the spec layer has shifted to a different
catalog entry, and the calibration has to follow.

## How long can a chain get before it's illegible?

The catalog says nothing about this — modifier-product composition closes for
arbitrarily long chains. Legibility is a UI question, but it has a math shadow: the
chain's _steps_ need to be inspectable for the player to read them. Chains that aren't
inspectable lose their educational value; players can't reason about which upgrade
matters most because they can't see what each upgrade does.

Working answers from the corpus:

- **Up to ~5 steps with no UI scaffolding.** A pipeline like "base × (1 + boost) ×
  prestige × tickspeed × milestone" can be presented as a single formula and players
  read it. Cookie Clicker's per-building output sits in this regime.
- **5–20 steps with a typed pipeline UI.** Profectus / Prestige Tree-style
  modifier-chain displays — each step named, each contribution visible, each step's
  effect on the running total shown. Players can hover and see the chain. The
  [modifier chains modern page](/modern/modifier-chains) walks the design pattern.
- **Past 20 steps, organized by category.** Synergism-scale stacks group modifiers
  by category (additive multipliers, multiplicative multipliers, exponent
  multipliers, achievement bonuses, prestige bonuses…) and present them as
  collapsible sections. The chain is still inspectable, but only at a higher level
  of organization. Each Synergism currency owns a typed array of named contributors
  reduced into one number — the array _is_ the chain, and the UI renders the same
  array directly:

  <Cite repo="SynergismOfficial" path="src/Statistics.ts" line="392-476" />

  ```ts
  export const allWowCubeStats: NumberStatLine[] = [
    { i18n: 'AscensionScore',   stat: () => Math.pow(.../3000, 1/4.1) },
    { i18n: 'GlobalCube',       stat: () => calculateAllCubeMultiplier() },
    { i18n: 'AchievementBonus', stat: () => +getAchievementReward('wowCubeGain') },
    // ... fourteen named contributors total ...
  ];

  export const calculateCubeMultiplier = () =>
    allWowCubeStats.reduce((a, b) => a * b.stat(), 1);
  ```

  The reduce is one line; the inspection surface is the array. Tesseracts,
  Hypercubes, Platonics, Hepteracts, and Octeracts each get their own array of
  similar depth, and the same shape extends to offerings,
  obtainium, ascension speed, and quark gain. The category split lives in
  _which array_ a contributor goes into; within each array the order is the chain.

The break-points aren't sharp; they're observations from the corpus. What's
mathematically real is that each step is a coordinate change on the rate, and a
designer who builds a 50-step chain has to decide what _unit_ of inspection to ship.
Step-by-step inspection at 50 steps is unreadable; collapsed inspection by category
is readable but loses per-upgrade visibility. The trade-off is the design call.

## When does a modifier become a soft cap?

A specific case worth flagging: a modifier that's nominally state-independent can
_effectively_ soft cap a rate when its value grows slower than the rate it's modifying.
If $\mu_m$ is a multiplier that grows as $x^{0.5}$ while the rate grows as $x^2$, the
_effective_ rate the player sees is $x^{2.5}$ — which is fine — but the _contribution_
of the multiplier shrinks as a fraction of total. From the player's perspective, the
multiplier eventually stops mattering. From the math's perspective, the multiplier is
still doing its job; it's just that the relative shape it bestows is asymptotically
small.

This is the same diagnosis as the obsolete-mechanic case at the modifier level. The
fix is the same: change the composition. A multiplier whose growth rate is too slow
relative to what it's multiplying needs to either grow faster (different rate function)
or compose differently (modify a different state, possibly a downstream prestige
state instead of the immediate production state).

The genre's working response is to chain modifiers carefully: each modifier in the
chain should be growing fast enough to keep its contribution from collapsing. When a
modifier feels useless, it usually isn't broken — it's growing too slowly for the
scale it's being applied at. The math diagnosis runs faster than playtesting does.

## Where the math stops

The catalog's modifier-product rule closes for any state-independent multiplier and
diagnoses where state-dependent ones go. It doesn't decide:

- **Which upgrades should be additive vs multiplicative.** Both are valid; the
  choice is design, downstream of pacing and progression-shape decisions the math
  has already established.
- **How to _present_ the chain.** Inspectable chain UIs are a design pattern, not a
  mathematical requirement. Some games hide the chain entirely and present only the
  final rate; the math doesn't care, the player experience does.
- **How "impactful" a modifier feels.** A modifier's _math contribution_ (its
  multiplicative factor) and its _felt impact_ (the visible jump in production
  numbers) are not the same thing. A 1.5× multiplier that takes the player from
  10/sec to 15/sec feels enormous; the same 1.5× taking them from 1e50 to 1.5e50
  feels invisible. The math is the same; the felt impact is downstream of how the
  numbers are formatted and what comparisons the player has on screen. UI work, not
  curve work.

Modifier chains are where math and UI design meet most directly in the genre. The
math says what the chain _is_; the UI says what the chain _looks like_. Both have to
be designed; only one of them has closed-form answers.
