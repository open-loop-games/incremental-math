# Pacing — targeting milestones

The most universal designer question: how fast should the game move? Pacing is what
turns a clean rate function into a felt experience, and it has more closed-form math
behind it than almost any other design surface. The doubling time on a geometric ladder
is one number, $(\ln 2)/\ln m$, and that one number sets nearly everything about the
feel of the late game.

This page bundles the questions where doubling time, first-passage targeting, and
asymptotic concentration combine into prescriptive answers. Most of the math lives on
the [exponential family](/rate-functions/exponential) catalog page; what's here is the
design-side reading.

## How do I pick a doubling time?

The math: a geometric cost ladder $\text{cost}(n) = a \cdot m^n$ has a doubling time of
$d = (\ln 2)/\ln m$ purchases. Pick $m$ and you've picked $d$ — the two numbers are
the same number in different units. Cookie Clicker's $m = 1.15$ gives $d \approx 4.96$;
roughly each five purchases double the cost.

"$1.15$ ≈ doubles every five" became the genre default because it produces a felt
cadence: a few minutes between visible cost jumps in the early game, a session-length
worth of work between them in the late game, and the player notices the ratio without
having to read formulas.

The design-side question is "how many doublings do I want a session of my game to
include?" Pick that number — call it $K$. A typical session involves the player
purchasing some target count $N$ of a building. The doubling count is $N/d$. So
$d = N/K$, and $m = 2^{1/d} = 2^{K/N}$.

Concrete: if a session has the player buy 30 of a building and you want them to feel
~6 doublings of cost in that session, $d = 5$ and $m \approx 2^{1/5} \approx 1.149$ —
which is exactly why $1.15$ feels right for sessions of that shape. The genre default
isn't arbitrary; it's the answer to a specific session-length question. A different
session shape gives a different answer.

The catalog's [exponential family page](/rate-functions/exponential) has the
[live calculator](/rate-functions/exponential#geometric-discrete) — slide $m$, watch doubling time and
production curves update together.

## Targeting a beat time directly

The doubling-time question — "$K$ doublings across $N$ purchases" — assumes the
designer already thinks in doublings. A different framing: "I want a $60$-second
beat at level $10$, with the first purchase taking $10$ seconds." Both produce the
same $m$ in the end; the math just runs in the other direction.

The closed form is the inverse of the geometric ladder. With base beat time $T_1$,
target beat time $T_n$, and target level $n$:

$$T_n = T_1 \cdot m^{n-1} \implies m = \left(\frac{T_n}{T_1}\right)^{1/(n-1)}$$

This is the same number as the doubling-time formula gives. The advantage of the
inverted form is that it accepts inputs designers already know how to specify — "I
want my level-10 purchases to take a minute" is a more intuitive starting point than
"I want six doublings across thirty levels." The result is the same multiplier,
named differently. Use whichever framing matches the way the design conversation
actually goes.

## What beat time should I target?

The wizard above takes a target beat time as an input but doesn't say where the
target should come from. The corpus has a working answer: target beat times that
fall in [Csikszentmihalyi's flow channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(psychology)).
The named bands map to incremental beat times as follows:

- **Arousal** (under $30$s): scanning for the next action; little strategic depth.
- **Flow** ($30$s – $2$ min): time to plan and feel; the engaged zone.
- **Flow upper** ($2$ – $5$ min): still engaged, beginning to stretch.
- **Boredom** ($5$ – $15$ min): looking for prestige or a new mechanic.
- **Frustration** ($15$ min – $1$ hr): the wait stops feeling like progress.
- **Breakdown** (over $1$ hr): pure idle territory.

The closed form for the multiplier range that keeps a given level in a given channel
is the inverse of the geometric ladder. With base beat $T_1$ and target level $n$,
to keep $T(n) \in [T_{\min}, T_{\max}]$:

$$\left(\frac{T_{\min}}{T_1}\right)^{1/(n-1)} \le m \le \left(\frac{T_{\max}}{T_1}\right)^{1/(n-1)}$$

For $T_1 = 10$s and $n = 10$, the Flow band $[30, 120]$ resolves to
$m \in [1.13, 1.32]$. The genre default $1.15$ lands comfortably inside that range —
Cookie Clicker and the games that copied its ratio sit there because their designers,
deliberately or otherwise, picked an $m$ that puts level-10 beats in Flow.

The bands aren't binding constraints. Plenty of games target Arousal (the gentler
ratios of mobile idle, like the $1.07$ Pecorella records for AdVenture Capitalist's
first business) or push into Boredom on purpose (deep-prestige walls
designed to force a reset). What the channel mapping does is _name_ what a given $m$
is doing to the beat curve, and which level the curve crosses each channel boundary.
"We're in Flow at level 10 but Boredom by level 20" is a more useful design
conversation than "the curve gets steep around there."

## How long should the early game be?

This is two questions stacked. "What's the duration of the early game?" is a
first-passage problem: time to threshold $C_{\text{early-end}}$. "Where do I put
$C_{\text{early-end}}$?" is the design call the formula cannot choose. Pacing math
answers the first; the threshold is a tuning decision.

The first part: with rate $r$ and cumulative $R(t)$, the time to threshold $C$ is
$T(C) = R^{-1}(C)$, which the catalog gives in closed form for every entry. For a
geometric ladder, $T$ is logarithmic in $C$ — the time to a milestone $K$ levels in is
proportional to $K$, not to $m^K$. The early game's _duration_ in real time scales
linearly with how many levels you call "early."

The second part is taste. The genre's working answer for clickers is that the early
game ends when the first major upgrade unlocks ("first prestige" or "first major
multiplier"), and that upgrade typically arrives 5–15 minutes in. A doubling-time
calculation backs into the right $m$ given that target. For session-paced games the
early game is shorter; for slow-burn idle the early game can be hours.

What the math forbids: a geometric ladder can't have a _short_ early game and a _short_
late game both. The doubling structure is uniform — pick $m$ and you've picked the
ratio of late-game pacing to early-game pacing. Games that want the early game to feel
fast and the late game to feel deep need either a [piecewise composition](/rate-functions/composition#piecewise-splice)
of two geometrics (different $m$ before and after a threshold) or a different rate
function entirely. The Pecorella-style single-$m$ ladder is uniformly paced by
construction.

## How do I prevent the late game from being a wall?

Re-read [the asymptotic concentration lemma](/rate-functions/exponential#asymptotic-concentration): for a
geometric cumulative, the last $K$ levels carry approximately $1 - m^{-K}$ of the total
integral. For $m = 1.15$ and $K = 10$, that's 75%. The math is doing this, regardless
of what the rest of the game does — the _feeling_ that the last few levels of any
building are the whole grind is mathematically true.

The design-side problem is what to _do_ about it. Three working answers:

- **Soft cap the cost growth.** Replace the geometric ladder with a piecewise
  composition: $m = 1.15$ early, $m = 1.05$ past threshold $t$. The post-threshold
  doubling time is now $\approx 14$ purchases instead of $\approx 5$, and the
  late-game "wall" gets a slope change rather than a slope continuation. The
  [bending the curve](/design-playbook/bending) page covers the design choices.
- **Add a multiplicative modifier that grows with progress.** A multiplier on the
  rate doesn't change the _shape_ of the cost cumulative but it _rescales the
  threshold_ — players reach the same level for less effective cost. Each prestige
  layer in modern games does this; it's why prestige is the genre's main pacing lever.
- **Cap the level entirely with a milestone gate.** A hard cap turns the runaway into
  a reachable endpoint. Players know when they're done. This changes the game's shape
  fundamentally — it's no longer a pacing decision, it's an end-of-content decision.
  Most idle games avoid this for a reason; some lean into it.

What the math says is that some response is _required_ if you want the late game to
feel like progress and not like a wall. A pure $m = 1.15$ ladder with no modulation will
end at "you waited 75% of total time for the last 10 levels of one building." Whether
that's a wall or a feature depends on what the game wants those 10 levels to _be_; if
the answer is "the climactic moment of the run," it's a feature, and if it's "another
20-minute grind," it's a wall.

## When is m = 1.15 wrong?

The genre default is right for sessions involving roughly 30 purchases of a building
across a 5–15 minute play window. It's wrong (or at least, suboptimal) for any session
shape outside that range:

- **Long-form idle (multi-hour sessions).** If a session spans 100+ levels of a
  building, $m = 1.15$ produces a runaway that hits float-overflow in the late game.
  Either drop $m$ (1.05–1.10 is more common for idle-heavy games) or
  [piecewise it](/rate-functions/composition#piecewise-splice) past a threshold.
- **Short bursts (sub-5-minute sessions).** Players don't see enough doublings to feel
  the cadence; the mechanic feels static. Crank $m$ — 1.25–1.35 — so a short session
  contains 2–3 doublings.
- **Games where buildings aren't the main loop.** If the headline progression is
  prestige currency or an idle multiplier rather than building purchases, the
  doubling time of the _displayed currency_ is what matters. The cost ladder of a
  side-system can be picked independently to fit _its_ role in the loop.
- **Games that ship a heavy tickspeed system.** If tickspeed is itself a major
  multiplier on the rate, then the _effective_ doubling time the player feels is
  shortened by the tickspeed compounding. AD's tickspeed upgrade uses a per-purchase
  ratio of about 10, and because tickspeed compounds into every dimension's output,
  the effective doubling time the player feels is far shorter than the dimensions'
  own cost ratios suggest.

The pattern: $1.15$ is the answer to one specific question about session shape. If your
game has a different session shape, it has a different $m$, and reading the genre
default as a universal best practice is taking someone else's answer to your problem.

## Where the math stops

Pacing math diagnoses _cadence_ — how fast the curve climbs and how long milestones
take. Pacing math doesn't decide:

- **Whether the cadence is fun.** Some audiences love five-minute doublings; some
  want hour-long ones. The math quantifies the cadence; the audience decides whether
  the cadence is right for them.
- **What the player does between purchases.** The first-passage time is a duration in
  real time; what _fills_ that duration — clicks, automation, mini-games, narrative —
  is content shape, not curve shape. Pacing math does not choose it.
- **How to telegraph the next milestone.** Players need to _see_ the next milestone
  approaching for the cadence to feel like progress. The HUD design, the progression
  bars, the unlock animations are all about making the math _legible_. Legibility is
  a UI problem.

Pacing math is the strongest tool the playbook has — almost every pacing question
has a closed-form answer — but it doesn't substitute for playtesting. The math gets
you to a defensible starting point, and playtesting tells you if that point is fun.
