# Multi-resource — lattices and the triangular escape

Modern incrementals rarely run a single currency. Production, prestige, side
mechanics, milestone rewards, and event currencies stack into resource lattices that
the player navigates simultaneously. The catalog's
[multi-resource extension](/rate-functions/composition) tells you when those lattices
stay in closed form and when they don't, and the answer is almost always "they're
triangular."

This page bundles the questions that come up when designing multi-currency systems:
when to keep currencies separate vs unify them, when conversion ratios stay tractable,
and what to do when resources start feeding back into each other in cycles the math
can't close.

## When should currencies be separate vs unified?

The math diagnosis: separate currencies are tractable when each currency's rate
function depends on a _different_ state. The system [decouples](/rate-functions/composition)
and each currency is its own first-passage problem in isolation. When two currencies'
rates depend on the same state, the system _couples_, and closure depends on the
shape of the coupling.

The design call:

- **Separate, decoupled.** The cleanest case. Two currencies with no shared state.
  Each one accumulates against its own clock, has its own milestones, and is
  manipulated by its own upgrades. Math-wise this is two independent first-passage
  problems running side by side. The risk is the [obsolete-mechanic](./composition)
  problem: if one currency's rate outgrows the other's, the slower one becomes
  irrelevant unless it converts back into the faster.
- **Separate but [triangular](/rate-functions/composition).** The most common case in
  shipped games. Currency A produces currency B; currency B is a modifier on
  currency A's rate or feeds upgrades that improve currency A's mechanics. The
  dependency runs one way, so the system is solvable in dependency order: solve A
  with its current modifiers, derive B from A's accumulation, apply B's effect on
  the next iteration of A. Math-wise this stays in the algebra as long as each
  layer is elementary.
- **Unified into one currency.** When the design question is "do these two values
  represent the same kind of thing?" and the answer is yes, unifying is cleanest.
  Cookie Clicker has cookies and Heavenly Chips, but only because they represent
  _different_ things (current-run accumulation vs cross-run prestige). If both
  represented "produced cookies," they'd be one number.

What the math forbids: bidirectional coupling between rates that doesn't decouple
into a triangular system. If currency A's rate depends on B and B's rate depends on
A in a way that doesn't reduce to one-direction dependency, the system is a coupled
ODE and lives in the [bifurcation rows](/rate-functions/composition#what-doesnt-compose-cleanly).
Most modern games avoid this for exactly the reason the math suggests: the system
becomes hard to balance, hard to predict, and hard to ship without spreadsheet
calibration the math layer can't help with.

## When does a conversion ratio stay closed-form?

The math: a conversion $H = f(C)$ that maps cumulative $C$ to a derived value $H$ stays
closed-form when $f$ is in the catalog. The
[catalog's composition rules](/rate-functions/composition#functional-composition) cover
the conditions: $f$ must be monotone elementary and its inverse must be elementary.
Cube root, square root, log, polynomial — all good. Sums of elementary functions —
good for the cumulative, but the inverse may need numerical methods. Lambert-W
inverses — closed-form formally, but lose interpretive transparency.

The design-side reading: pick a conversion that the catalog has named, and the
math will behave. Pick a conversion that's a sum or product of named conversions, and
the math will mostly behave but the inverse may need a calibration table. Pick a
conversion that's _outside_ the catalog, and the math probably won't close, and
shipping the conversion will involve solver code the algebra can't help with.

The corpus's working defaults — cube root, square root, log of cumulative — are all
catalog entries. Games that ship more exotic conversions usually carry the
calibration cost (named tables, hard-coded breakpoints, or numerical solvers in the
upgrade-affordability calculation).

## How do I design a triangular dependency that stays tractable?

The catalog's [triangular systems rule](/rate-functions/composition) says: order the
resources $R_1, \dots, R_k$ such that $R_i$'s rate depends only on $R_1, \dots, R_i$.
The system is solvable in sequence — solve $R_1$ first, substitute its solution into
$R_2$'s equation, solve, substitute into $R_3$'s, and so on. Closure of the whole
system reduces to closure of each scalar.

Design-side, this is the recipe most modern games follow:

- **Primary currency at the bottom.** Cookies, antimatter, paperclips. Its rate
  depends on counts of buildings and various multipliers. No back-feed from higher
  tiers in the _current_ run.
- **Building counts at the next level.** Each building's count grows as a function
  of the primary currency (you spend it to buy them). The dependency is one-way:
  buildings consume primary currency, don't produce it directly except through
  their per-tick output.
- **Prestige currency above that.** Heavenly Chips, Eternity Points. Its rate
  depends on the primary currency's cumulative across runs, and feeds back into the
  primary currency's rate via prestige multipliers. Within a single run, the
  dependency is one-way; _across_ runs, it loops, but the loop is at the cycle
  boundary, not in the rate function.
- **Layered prestige currencies above that.** Each layer is a function of the
  layer below's cumulative. Same triangular pattern, iterated.

The pattern stays tractable because the dependency is layered. Each scalar in the
sequence solves on its own; the system as a whole is the iterated chain. What
_breaks_ triangularity is any rate function that depends on a layer _above_ it
within a single run — a building whose output depends on the prestige currency the
player will earn at the end of the run, for example, would create a circular
reference that doesn't close.

The genre's working solution: keep the across-run feedback at the cycle boundary
(prestige conversion at end of run), not in the per-tick rate function. Each run is
triangular; the feedback happens between runs. This is exactly the
[functional composition](/rate-functions/composition) structure, applied to the
multi-resource case.

## What happens when resources have circular dependencies?

The honest answer: the math gets harder fast.

Two resources whose rates mutually depend on each other — the canonical example is
[Lotka-Volterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka%E2%80%93Volterra_equations)
predator-prey, but the genre versions are simpler: "currency A's production depends
on B, and B's production depends on A" — produce coupled ODEs that close only in
special cases. The catalog covers the special cases:

- **Linear coupling**. $dR/dt = AR + b$ for constant matrix $A$. Closes via the
  [matrix exponential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_exponential). Solvable
  for any $A$, but the formulas are dense; useful when the system is genuinely
  linear.
- **Conserved quantities**. Some coupled systems admit a conserved quantity
  $H(R) = \text{const}$. The trajectory is constrained to a level set of $H$,
  which reduces the dimension by one. Lotka-Volterra has this.
- **Decoupling via substitution**. If a clever change of variables converts the
  coupled system to a triangular one, the math closes again. Worth trying when the
  coupling looks like a candidate for substitution.

When none of these apply, the system is in the bifurcation rows and the spec layer
shifts to _qualitative_ descriptions: stable equilibria, basins of attraction,
periodic orbits. Quantitative timing requires numerical integration, but the spec
layer can still describe shape.

The design call: most circular dependencies in shipped incrementals are accidents.
A designer who didn't realize the coupling was circular ships a system that's
hard to balance, hard to predict, and hard to ship. The fix is almost always to
break the cycle: make the dependency triangular by introducing a delay (B uses
_last tick's_ A rather than current A), an aggregation (B depends on A's
_cumulative_, not A's _rate_), or a separation (one of the resources is moved to a
different layer that only feeds at cycle boundaries).

When the circular dependency is intentional — predator-prey ecosystem, supply-and-demand
economic loop — the design is committing to the bifurcation. The math the spec layer
runs in is no longer the catalog; it's the named adjacent math (linear ODE theory,
phase plane analysis, conserved quantities). That's a real investment to ship; the
games that do this successfully usually treat the coupled system as the _headline_
mechanic rather than as a side surface.

## How do I balance multiple resources?

Triangularity is half the answer; the other half is making the _dependencies_ between
layers feel meaningful to the player. Three patterns the corpus uses:

- **Each layer modifies the layer below.** Prestige currency multiplies primary
  currency rate; secondary currency unlocks upgrades that multiply primary; layered
  prestige does the same recursively. Players experience the layers as connected
  because each one _visibly_ affects the layer below.
- **Each layer gates content the layer below can't reach.** Some upgrades cost
  primary currency _and_ secondary currency; some require a milestone in both. The
  triangular dependency is reflected in the upgrade tree's structure.
- **Each layer's progression is felt in the layer below's pacing.** A run with
  more prestige currency is visibly faster. A new prestige tier visibly compresses
  the time-to-milestone of the lower layer. The math is doing this; the design has
  to _show_ it.

Failure modes — multi-resource systems that feel "tacked on" rather than integrated —
usually have one of two diagnoses:

- **The currencies don't compose mathematically.** Two currencies that each have
  their own first-passage problem with no cross-feeding are running parallel
  games. Math says: triangulate them.
- **The currencies compose, but the player can't see it.** The math is right, but
  the UI doesn't show how the prestige multiplier shortened the next run, or how
  the secondary currency unlocked the upgrade in the primary tree. Math says:
  this is correct, fix the UI.

The diagnoses are different fixes. The math layer can run the first one fast (look
at the dependency graph; ship a triangular structure). The second one is UI work
that the math is silent about.

## Where the math stops

The catalog tells you when a multi-resource system stays in the algebra (decoupled,
triangular, or linear-coupled with constant coefficients), and what named adjacent
math takes over when it doesn't. It doesn't tell you:

- **Which resources to ship.** Whether your game has cookies and Heavenly Chips,
  or just cookies, or cookies and Heavenly Chips and a side currency for events,
  is a content decision. The math runs once you've decided which currencies exist.
- **What conversion rates to pick.** "$H = C^{1/3}$" is a catalog entry; "$H = C^{1/4}$"
  is also a catalog entry. The math is silent about which exponent fits your game's
  pacing intent. That's a session-shape and prestige-feel call, downstream of the
  math.
- **How to surface multi-resource state in the UI.** The mathematical fact of
  triangular dependency doesn't dictate how to show it. Modifier-chain UIs,
  resource-bar layouts, multi-currency HUDs, prestige tree visualizations — all are
  design choices the math is silent about.

Multi-resource systems are where the catalog's vocabulary is most directly useful
for _diagnosing_ problems with a design that's already partially built. "Why does
this currency feel useless?" almost always answers to "it's running its own
first-passage problem, decoupled from the loop the player cares about." "Why is
this system unstable?" almost always answers to "circular dependency you didn't
intend, which dropped the system out of triangular structure." The math runs
diagnoses fast; the fixes are the design work.
