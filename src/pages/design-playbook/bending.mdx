# Bending the curve — softcaps and regime breaks

Softcaps are how the genre patches the runaway tendency of unbounded geometric
growth. They're also where the most common design mistake is: the wrong shape, the
wrong threshold, or the wrong stack of caps will trade a runaway problem for a wall
problem. Math diagnoses both.

This page bundles the questions where the [softcap design pattern](/modern/softcaps)
meets the [power-law](/rate-functions/power-law) and [log-frontier](/rate-functions/log-frontier)
catalog entries. The Modern section's softcaps page walks the patterns the genre uses;
this page walks the design call that picks among them.

## When should I softcap?

The math diagnosis: when an unbounded rate function is producing a cumulative that
_nothing downstream consumes meaningfully_. If the cumulative stops corresponding to
a meaningful progression value past some threshold — because the player can't spend
it, or because spending it has [diminishing returns](/rate-functions/power-law#polynomial-rate) the
designer didn't intend — the rate function has outgrown its purpose, and a softcap
is the answer.

The negative diagnosis is just as important: if the rate's runaway _is_ the meaningful
progression — a late-game cookie count climbing past anything the player can read is
the _point_ of the late game, not a bug — softcapping is the wrong move. The math
doesn't say "all unbounded progression must be capped." It says "if the cumulative isn't
delivering value past a threshold, cap it there."

A clean test the design call can run: does the player notice when one
unit of the runaway value becomes available? If yes (still feels like progress), the
rate function is doing its job; don't cap. If no (the marginal unit arrives silently
because the formatter rounds it to invisibility, or because the next target is
priced past where the player can read), the rate has outgrown its UI, and capping it
either changes the shape or changes the formatter — but something must change.

## Which shape — root, log, hyperbolic, piecewise?

Each shape is in the catalog at a specific entry. Picking among them is picking
which property matters most for the cap's role.

**Root / fractional exponent**. $g(x, t) = t \cdot (x/t)^p$ for $0 < p < 1$. Is at the
[polynomial entry](/rate-functions/power-law#polynomial-rate) with fractional exponent. The post-cap
growth is sublinear but unbounded, with a smooth slope change at the threshold. Pick
this when the cap's role is "slow down growth, don't ceiling it." The exponent $p$
controls aggressiveness — $p = 0.5$ is the genre-default sqrt softcap, $p = 0.3$ is
harsh, $p = 0.7$ is gentle.

**Log**. $g(x, t) = t + k \cdot \log_{10}(x - t + 1)$. Is near the
[logarithmic entry](/rate-functions/log-frontier#logarithmic-rate). The post-cap growth is _very_
sublinear — every $10\times$ in input adds a fixed $k$ to output. Pick this when the
cap's role is "compress many orders of magnitude into a small number." Games that
stack many log caps use this shape because their underlying values span dozens of
orders of magnitude and need to end up on a stat the player can read.

**Hyperbolic / asymptotic**. $g(x) = L \cdot x / (x + k)$. Is at the
[rational entry $p > 1$](/rate-functions/power-law#rational--hyperbolic-rate) when taken as a cumulative. The
post-cap growth approaches a _finite ceiling_ $L$ that's never reached. Pick this when
the cap's role is "this stat represents a fraction or efficiency that should saturate
near 100%." Civ Clicker's cleric piety formula puts the Pyramid bonus through this
shape — "approach but never exceed 1.0" is the design intent the algebra encodes,
and the whole softcap is one inline subexpression:

<Cite repo="civ-clicker" path="scripts/civclicker.js" line="2396-2407" />

```js
function doClerics() {
  var pietyEarned = (
    civData.cleric.owned
    * (civData.cleric.efficiency + (civData.cleric.efficiency * (civData.writing.owned)))
    * (1 + ((civData.secrets.owned)
    * (1 - 100 / (civData.graveyard.owned + 100))))
    * curCiv.morale.efficiency
    * getWonderBonus(civData.piety)
  );
  // ...
}
```

The hyperbolic modifier is `1 - 100 / (graveyard + 100)`, the canonical
$1 - b/(b + x)$ shape with $b = 100$. At zero graveyards the bonus is zero;
at 100 graveyards it's $0.5$; at infinity it's $1.0$. The other modifiers in the
chain are state-independent multipliers — the page's
[modifier-product rule](/rate-functions/composition#modifier-product-state-independent-multipliers) — so the saturation is entirely
in this one parenthesized expression.

**Piecewise / regime split**. Different rate or growth on each side of a threshold,
glued continuously. Is at the [piecewise splice](/rate-functions/composition#piecewise-splice)
rule. Pick this when the cap's role is "change the regime visibly so the player feels
the transition." A pre/post-prestige cost shift built as two geometrics spliced is
the common instance: the player crosses a named threshold and the game's pacing
visibly changes.
Antimatter Dimensions ships the same shape as a parameterized class:

<Cite repo="AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode" path="src/core/math.js" line="320-352" />

```js
window.ExponentialCostScaling = class ExponentialCostScaling {
  // baseCost B, baseIncrease C, costScale S, purchasesBeforeScaling n.
  // Pre-scaling: B, B·C, B·C², ..., B·C^n.
  // Post-scaling: B·C^(n+1), B·C^(n+2)·S, B·C^(n+3)·S^3, ...
};
```

Pre-threshold is a pure geometric ladder $B \cdot C^k$; post-threshold the per-step
ratio shifts to $C \cdot S^{2k}$, a faster-growing geometric. The class
parameterizes the threshold as `purchasesBeforeScaling`, so a single object captures
both regimes plus the gluing point.

The picks are not interchangeable. Root and power are smooth and continuous-derivative
at the threshold; log is smooth but the slope change is sharper; piecewise has a
visible kink; hyperbolic has a hard ceiling. The
[softcaps comparison demo](/modern/softcaps#comparing-the-shapes) plots them on a shared axis if you want
the visual.

## How many caps should stack?

The math: cascading softcaps with successively harsher exponents at successively
higher thresholds is a piecewise splice where each regime applies a more aggressive
shape than the last. Closure is automatic per regime; inversion still works as long as
each cap closes individually.

The design-side question is "how many threshold crossings do I want a session to
contain?" Each crossing is a felt event for the player — a moment where the curve's
character visibly changes. Too few and the late game feels uniform; too many and the
player loses track of which cap is currently binding.

Working answers from the corpus:

- **One cap, one threshold.** Most early softcap implementations. Late-game has one
  obvious "wall" the player aims at and breaks through. Simple, legible, but the
  late-late-game often runs out of structure.
- **Two softcaps.** Early cap for "first slowdown," late cap for "the real wall."
  Common when the game has one major prestige layer.
- **Many caps, named individually.** Prestige Tree, Synergism. Each cap is
  addressable by name; upgrades target specific caps; challenges swap cap shapes.
  The pattern works because the game's _content_ loops around the caps — knowing
  which cap is binding is a real piece of late-game knowledge. Three or more caps
  starts to require this kind of UI scaffolding.

The catalog says nothing about which of these is right for a given game; it just says
each is a piecewise splice that closes. The design call is content-shaped: how
much does your late game depend on the caps as named features versus as background
shape?

## Can the curve un-bend?

The piecewise splice rule is symmetric — nothing in the math forbids the
post-threshold regime from being _gentler_ than the pre-threshold regime. The genre
default is that softcaps make later progress harder, but that's a convention, not a rule.

Fundamental ships a clean non-monotonic curve. Six stages, each generator with its own
per-purchase cost ratio; the spread within a stage is what sets that stage's feel:

| Stage | Cost ratios per generator       | Feel                          |
| ----- | ------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 1     | $1.40$ throughout               | moderate                      |
| 2     | $1.15$–$1.40$, last generator $4$ | moderate, one aggressive outlier |
| 3     | $1.15$–$1.35$, last two $10$     | harsh — peak wall             |
| 4     | $1.40$–$2$                      | moderate-high                 |
| 5     | $2$, $2$, then $1.11$           | gentle — valley on the final generator |
| 6     | $1.40$                          | moderate                      |

Stage 3 is the climactic difficulty wall: per-purchase cost ratio jumps to $10\times$,
which under the [doubling-time formula](/design-playbook/pacing#how-do-i-pick-a-doubling-time)
means the beat time at purchase $n$ is $10^{n-1}$ times the beat time at purchase 1. Stage 5
is the inverse — at $1.11$, per-purchase pacing visibly relaxes below the game's $1.40$
default. The valley appears as a breather _because_ the peak existed. Three conditions
seem to make non-monotonic work: a peak with strong prestige compression so the wall
isn't experienced as the end of progress, a valley that's meaningfully gentler than its
neighbours (a 5% change in cost ratio isn't felt), and a legible stage structure so
the player knows the curve changed shape. The math says nothing about which side of a
threshold is steeper; the convention exists because gentle-after-harsh is rare enough
that most designers don't think to author it.

## Hard cap versus softcap

Mathematically: a hard cap has a discontinuous derivative at the cap (slope drops
from positive to zero); a softcap keeps a positive marginal return everywhere. The
catalog's vocabulary makes this concrete — both are functions, both are integrable,
but the hard cap's first-passage time goes from finite-and-keeps-growing to
infinite-past-the-cap, and the softcap's keeps growing finitely.

Design-side, the discontinuity is the central question. A hard cap _ends_ the
relevant progression — past the cap, the value stops responding to player action. The
genre's working consensus, with exceptions, is that hard caps on _progression_ values
end the relevant gameplay loop and should be avoided; hard caps on _resource
containers_ (slot count, queue depth, simultaneous-effect count) are fine because the
player can read them as content shape rather than as a wall.

When hard caps work for progression: when the _cap is the milestone_. Trimps awards
no helium before world 19; the hard cutoff is content, not a wall.
<Cite repo="Trimps" path="main.js" line="4194" /> The
player doesn't experience it as "I'm capped"; they experience it as "I can't earn
helium yet." The design distinction is whether the player is _trying_ to cross the
cap when they hit it. If yes (cap as wall), the discontinuity is felt as the wall it
is. If no (cap as gate), the discontinuity is invisible — the player isn't pushing
against it.

When softcaps work better than hard caps: any case where the player is actively
pushing against the cap as a target. The softcap's positive-marginal-return
preserves the rhythm of "click, see number rise" that idle games are built around;
the hard cap's zero-marginal-return breaks the rhythm. Modern incrementals overwhelmingly
ship soft for this reason.

## How do I make a softcap feel fair?

Three patterns the corpus uses, each one math-diagnosable:

- **Make it visible.** Players understand a cap they can see. A status indicator
  showing the cap is binding, a tooltip showing pre-cap and post-cap values, a
  named cap in the UI — all pull the cap from "invisible nerf the player feels but
  can't name" to "visible mechanic the player can plan against." The math doesn't
  care; the legibility is everything.
- **Make it removable, eventually.** A cap that the player can never break is felt as
  a permanent constraint; one they can break (with a milestone, an upgrade, a
  prestige) becomes a target. Most modern games offer "reduces softcap by X%"
  upgrades for exactly this reason. The math is just a parameter slide on the cap's
  threshold or strength; the felt experience is "the wall is moveable."
- **Make sure there's something to do past it.** A softcap regulates the _rate_ of
  progression past the threshold; it doesn't provide _content_ past the threshold.
  If the post-cap regime has nothing to do but watch a number tick up at a sublinear
  rate, the cap turns into the wall it was meant to avoid. Modern incrementals usually
  pair caps with side-systems that activate past the threshold — a milestone hunt, a
  new layer, a separate mechanic. The cap is the regulator; the engagement is
  a separate design surface.

The fairness call is downstream of all three. A softcap that's invisible, immovable,
and gates an empty post-cap regime will feel unfair regardless of which catalog shape
it uses. A softcap that's named, breakable, and gates real content will feel fair
even if the underlying shape is harsh.

## Where the math stops

The catalog tells you what each softcap shape does to the curve. It doesn't tell you:

- **Whether to cap at all.** As above — "is the post-cap regime serving the design?"
  is a content question, not a curve question.
- **Where to put the threshold.** The math diagnoses that the cap _needs_ a
  threshold; it's silent about whether the threshold should be at $10^6$ or $10^{50}$.
  That's a session-shape call about how many doublings of pre-cap progression you want
  the player to experience.
- **What the post-cap UI should say.** A capped value behaves differently from an
  uncapped one and the player needs to read the difference. Display layers, named
  cap indicators, telegraphed upcoming caps — all of that is UI work and the math is
  silent about it.

The catalog is at its best when the question is "what shape" or "how many" or "when
does this start being broken." It's at its worst when the question is "will this be
fun" or "where exactly should the breakpoint be" — those are observation problems, and
the only reliable answer is to play the curve at the threshold's neighborhood and see
how it feels.
